<Before>
Animal power is rented, not owned. A donkey turning a mill has to be fed every day of the year
whether or not there is grain to grind, it works a few hours before it needs rest, and it needs a
handler for every hour it works. For a Roman bakery that is an acceptable overhead. For a village
it is often not, which means the household quern and several hours of somebody's day. Meanwhile
every stream in Europe runs downhill continuously, for nothing, doing no work at all.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Vitruvius describes a vertical waterwheel driving a millstone through gearing in the first century
BCE, and the Romans build them at scale — the complex at Barbegal near Arles runs sixteen wheels in
two banks down a hillside. What happens in this period is not the invention but the spread. Between
roughly 400 and 1000 the watermill goes from a Mediterranean engineering set piece to standard
village infrastructure across Europe and the Islamic world, grinding grain, fulling cloth, sawing
timber and driving forge hammers. The Domesday survey of 1086 counts 5,624 mills in England.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="2–3 hp · 5,624 mills in Domesday · 24 hours a day">
A waterwheel extracts energy in one of two ways, and the difference is large. An undershot wheel
sits in the current and is pushed by the water's momentum, which is simple to install and recovers
perhaps a fifth of the available energy. An overshot wheel takes water onto the top of the wheel in
buckets and is turned by its weight as it falls, which needs a leat and a head of water but
recovers more than half. The practical output of a working medieval mill is two to three
horsepower, four or five times a donkey and forty times a person.

The number that actually matters is not the power but the duty cycle. The wheel turns every hour of
every day the water runs, and the marginal cost of the next hour is nothing. What it costs instead
is capital and place: a dam, a leat, a wheel, a gear train, and a site on a stream with usable fall.
That fixes industry to geography for the next thousand years.

<Forward year="500">
The mill is the first machine most people ever see, and the first case of a settlement organising
itself around a power source rather than the other way round. Villages grow at the fall of water,
and the miller's fee is one of the oldest recorded industrial charges. The dependence on geography
is total, and the next two entries on this road are both ways of escaping it: wind, where no river
falls <Modern>(<Ref to="the-windmill">the windmill</Ref>, P.IV)</Modern>, and finally a fire that
makes its own motion, which can be built anywhere at all <Modern>(<Ref
to="newcomen-engine">the Newcomen engine</Ref>, P.VI)</Modern>.
</Forward>

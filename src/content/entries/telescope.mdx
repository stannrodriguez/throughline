<Before>
Astronomy before 1608 is naked-eye astronomy, and it is very good. Tycho Brahe's positional
measurements are accurate to a couple of arcminutes, made with large graduated quadrants and no
optics at all. What no observer has is resolution: the eye's pupil is a few millimetres across, and
that sets a hard floor on how small a detail can be separated. Planets are points of light. The
Moon is a mottled disc. Whether Copernicus or Ptolemy is right is a question about which model fits
the same set of naked-eye positions more neatly, and both can be made to fit.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
In October 1608 Hans Lippershey, a spectacle-maker in Middelburg, applies to the States General of
the Netherlands for a patent on a device for seeing distant things as if nearby. The application is
refused on the grounds that the trick is too easy to copy, which is accurate — within a year
spyglasses of about three magnifications are being sold across Europe. Galileo hears a description
in 1609, works out the arrangement, and grinds his own to twenty and then thirty times. Between the
7th and the 13th of January 1610 he watches four small stars beside Jupiter change position from
night to night, and works out that they are going round it.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="3× Dutch · 20× Galileo · 4 moons, January 1610">
The Galilean telescope is two lenses: a weak convex objective that brings light to a focus, and a
strong concave eyepiece placed short of that focus, which throws the rays parallel again into the
eye. Magnification is the ratio of the two focal lengths. The objective also matters for a second
reason that is easier to overlook — it is much wider than a pupil, so it gathers far more light,
which is why faint objects appear at all.

The limit is the glass. Seventeenth-century optical glass carries bubbles and striations, and a
simple lens brings different colours to focus at slightly different points, so every image has a
coloured fringe. Galileo's answer is to stop the objective down and accept a narrow, dim field.
Better glass and achromatic doublets take another century and a half.

What the instrument actually settles is narrower than the usual claim. Four moons orbiting Jupiter
prove that not everything goes round the Earth, and the phases of Venus, which Galileo observes
later in 1610, are incompatible with Ptolemy's arrangement. Neither observation proves the Earth
moves; both rule out the model that had stood since antiquity.

<Forward year={1608}>
<em>Sidereus Nuncius</em> is published in March 1610, two months after the observations, and it is
a short book of drawings and dates rather than an argument. That is the shape of the period: an
artisan's device produces something a person can look at, and the theory arrives afterwards to
account for it. Galileo also spots the commercial application immediately and spends years trying
to sell it — four moons on a predictable schedule are a clock visible from anywhere on Earth, and a
clock is longitude. The method works on land and is hopeless from a rolling deck, which leaves the
problem for a watchmaker to solve a hundred and fifty years later <Modern>(<Ref
to="marine-chronometer">the marine chronometer</Ref>, P.VI)</Modern>.
</Forward>

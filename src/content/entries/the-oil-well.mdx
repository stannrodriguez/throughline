<Before>
Lighting in 1850 runs on whale oil, and the whales are being hunted out — sperm oil prices roughly
double over the decade as voyages lengthen from months to years. The alternatives are tallow
candles, which smoke, and coal gas, which requires a municipal plant and a pipe network and
therefore exists only in cities. Petroleum itself is known: it seeps to the surface in western
Pennsylvania, is skimmed off creeks, and is sold in small bottles as a medicine. Nobody has a way
of getting it in quantity, because nobody has tried to go down after it.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Edwin Drake, hired by a company formed to exploit the seeps, applies the salt-drilling trade's
methods to oil at Titusville. His contribution is the drive pipe: an iron pipe driven down to
bedrock through the loose gravel that had collapsed into every previous attempt, with the drill
working inside it. On 27 August 1859 the well strikes oil at 69.5 feet and produces around
twenty-five barrels a day. Within a year the valley holds hundreds of wells, and the first
refineries are distilling the crude into kerosene for lamps.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="69.5 ft · ~25 barrels a day · 42 MJ/kg against coal at 27">
Refining is fractional distillation: crude is heated and the vapours drawn off in bands by boiling
point, giving light gases, then naphtha and petrol, then kerosene, then heavier oils and finally
tar. In 1859 the valuable band is kerosene, because it burns in a lamp with a bright steady flame
and little smoke. Petrol is a dangerously volatile nuisance that early refiners dump into the
nearest creek.

The property that eventually matters more than any of that is energy density. Petroleum products
hold about 42 megajoules per kilogram against roughly 27 for coal, and being liquid they can be
pumped along a pipe, poured into a tank of any shape, and metered — none of which is true of a
solid that has to be shovelled. For a stationary engine beside a coalfield that is irrelevant. For
anything that has to carry its own fuel it is decisive, and it is why every vehicle that leaves the
rails runs on oil.

<Forward year={1859}>
The industry that grows out of Titusville is built to sell lamp oil, and its first market is
destroyed inside thirty years by electric light. What saves it is the fraction it had been throwing
away: a volatile liquid that vaporises readily and burns fast is exactly what an engine wants if the
combustion is happening inside the cylinder <Modern>(<Ref to="internal-combustion">internal
combustion</Ref>, P.IX)</Modern>. Petroleum then becomes the fuel of the twentieth century, and the
strip's energy-per-person figure roughly doubles between Period IX and Period X on the strength of
it.
</Forward>

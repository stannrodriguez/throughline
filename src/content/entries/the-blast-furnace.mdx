<Before>
Two and a half thousand years of iron-making have never produced liquid iron. The bloomery works in
the solid state: charcoal reduces the ore, slag melts and drains away, and what is left is a spongy
lump that has to be hammered for hours to consolidate. Every piece of iron in Europe is therefore
shaped by a smith beating it, which caps output at what arms can do and rules out any object too
large or too intricate to forge. Bronze can be poured into a mould and iron cannot, which is why
the best cannon are still made of an alloy that depends on imported tin.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The blast furnace is a taller shaft, charged continuously from the top with ore, charcoal and
limestone, and blown from below by bellows that a waterwheel works without stopping. China had
furnaces of this kind by the first century; in Europe they appear from the twelfth century onward,
with excavated examples at Lapphyttan in Sweden, and are established across the Rhineland and the
Weald by around 1350. The furnace runs for weeks at a time, and twice a day the workers tap it and
molten iron runs out into a sand mould.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="1,500° blast · 4% carbon · 1,150° melting point">
The obvious explanation is the temperature, and it is only half the story. Water-driven bellows
running continuously do lift the furnace from the bloomery's 1,250° to about 1,500°, which is a
genuine and hard-won hundred and fifty degrees. But pure iron melts at 1,538°, so even 1,500° would
not be enough on its own.

What closes the gap is carbon. Iron held for hours in intimate contact with burning charcoal
dissolves carbon into itself, and carbon depresses iron's melting point sharply: at three to four
percent, the alloy is liquid at around 1,150°. The furnace does not so much melt iron as make an
iron that is easier to melt. The limestone charged with the ore is there to combine with the
silica in the rock into a fluid slag that floats and can be raked off.

The product, cast iron, is a different material from anything a bloomery gives. It pours into fine
detail and is hard and cheap, and it is brittle: the carbon that let it melt sits in the solid metal
as graphite flakes that crack under a bending load. Turning it back into workable wrought iron needs
a second hearth, the finery, where a blast of air burns the carbon out again.

<Forward year="1350">
Poured iron is cheap iron, and the first thing Europe does with it is cast cannon — which in turn
makes the stone castle obsolete inside a century. The second thing, slower and larger, is that iron
becomes a construction material rather than a smith's product: rails, beams, pipes, frames and
machine parts all wait on a metal that can be produced by the tonne rather than the bar. The
furnace's remaining constraint is its fuel, because charcoal ties every ironworks to a forest, and
England runs short of forest first <Modern>(<Ref to="coke-smelted-iron">coke-smelted iron</Ref>,
P.VII)</Modern>.
</Forward>

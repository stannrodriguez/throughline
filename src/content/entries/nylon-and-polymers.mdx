<Before>
Chemistry before the 1920s has no settled account of what a plastic or a rubber or a protein
actually is. The prevailing view treats them as colloids — aggregates of small molecules clustered
by some unspecified force — because a molecule with tens of thousands of atoms in it seemed
absurd. Hermann Staudinger argues from 1920 that these substances are single covalent chains of
enormous length, and is ridiculed for it for a decade. Without that picture, improving a material
is necessarily empirical: mix things, heat them, see what happens, which is how vulcanised rubber
and celluloid and Bakelite were all arrived at.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
DuPont hires Wallace Carothers in 1928 to do fundamental research on polymerisation with no product
in view. Working from Staudinger's picture, his group builds long chains deliberately by
condensation — reacting two molecules that each have a reactive group at both ends, so the product
can react again at either end and the chain grows. On 28 February 1935 they make a polyamide from
hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid, six carbons in each, which the company calls nylon 6-6. It
melts around 265°, can be spun into fibre, and is announced in 1938 as made from coal, air and
water. Stockings reach the shops in May 1940 and sell sixty-four million pairs in the first year.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="28 February 1935 · nylon 6-6 · 64 million pairs in the first year">
Two steps make the material, and the second is as important as the first. Polymerisation gives long
molecules in a tangle, which is a tough plastic and a poor fibre. Drawing — stretching the extruded
filament to several times its length — pulls the chains into alignment along the fibre axis, where
hydrogen bonds between adjacent amide groups lock them to each other. Strength along the fibre
rises severalfold, because a load is now carried along covalent backbones rather than across the
weak points between tangled chains.

What is new here is the direction of work. Every material on this road until now was found and then
refined: a better ore, a better heat treatment, a better mix. Nylon is specified first — a chain
with these units, at this length, giving these properties — and then synthesised to that
specification. That is the method the rest of the century runs on, and it produces polyester,
polyethylene, epoxy, Kevlar and the resins that hold aerospace composites together.

<Forward year={1935}>
Carothers did not see any of it: severely depressed for years, he died by suicide in April 1937,
before nylon reached the market. The habit his work established is the one the materials road
carries to the end — that a material is a design problem, with the molecule as the thing being
designed. The next question after what the molecule is turns out to be how precisely it can be
placed, which is where the road ends up <Modern>(<Ref to="atomic-layer-deposition">atomic-layer
deposition</Ref>, P.XII)</Modern>.
</Forward>

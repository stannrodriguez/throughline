<Before>
Roman numerals are a fine notation for recording a quantity and a poor one for operating on it.
There is no column that means tens, so MCMXLIV cannot be added to anything by lining it up; the
value of a symbol depends on what stands beside it in a way that resists procedure. Ancient
arithmetic is therefore done on hardware — a counting board, an abacus, pebbles in grooves — and
the numerals record only the answer. The consequence is that nobody can check the working, because
there is no working. The beads have already been swept away.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The system comes from India, where Brahmagupta sets out rules for zero as a number in its own right
in 628. Around 825 the Baghdad scholar al-Khwarizmi writes a treatise on calculating with the
Hindu numerals, and a second on restoration and balancing whose title gives us the word algebra.
His name, through Latin translation, gives us the word algorithm. The system reaches Europe through
Islamic Spain and is argued about for centuries before Fibonacci's <em>Liber Abaci</em> of 1202
makes the commercial case for it in Italian.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="10 digits · base 10 · 628 CE for the zero">
Two ideas do the work. The first is place value: the position of a digit sets what it is worth, so
ten symbols suffice for any number of any size. The second is a symbol for an empty column, without
which place value is ambiguous — 205 and 25 are otherwise the same marks with a gap that scribes
may or may not preserve.

What follows from these is a set of algorithms. Adding, carrying, long multiplication and long
division become fixed sequences of steps performed on digits in columns, learnable by rote and
identical every time. That has two consequences. Anyone can be taught to compute without
understanding why the method works, which is what makes commercial arithmetic a mass skill. And
every step stays on the page, so an error can be found rather than merely suspected — an abacus
gives you an answer, a worked column gives you an argument.

<Forward year="825">
Written arithmetic is the quiet precondition for the next eight hundred years of quantitative work.
Double-entry bookkeeping, navigational tables, insurance, astronomy and gunnery all assume a
notation in which a long calculation can be carried out on paper and audited afterwards by someone
who was not there. It also makes arithmetic mechanisable: a procedure that is the same every time
is a procedure a device can execute, first with gears and later with switches <Modern>(<Ref
to="the-transistor">the transistor</Ref>, P.X)</Modern>. The zero that made it possible was the
hardest part to accept, and the Italian cities banned the new numerals for a while on the grounds
that they were easy to forge.
</Forward>

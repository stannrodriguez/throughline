<Before>
Watt has a patent, a working principle and no engine. His cylinders are cast and then bored with a
cutter on a bar supported at one end, which flexes and follows whatever irregularity the casting
already had, so the bore comes out oval, tapered or both. The gap between piston and wall is
packed with rope, rags, leather and tallow, and it still leaks: the steam Watt has gone to such
lengths to conserve escapes past the piston, and the engine that should save three-quarters of the
coal cannot be made to hold pressure. For six years after the patent, the separate condenser is a
good idea that does not work.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
John Wilkinson patents a machine in 1774 for boring cannon from a solid casting rather than casting
them hollow. Its principle is rigidity: the boring bar is supported at both ends and turns on its
own axis, while the cutter head is fed steadily along it, so the cut follows the bar's line instead
of the hole's existing shape. Applied to Watt's cylinders from 1775, it produces a fifty-inch bore
with error Watt recorded as not exceeding the thickness of an old shilling — something on the order
of a millimetre and a half across more than a metre of diameter. Wilkinson bored nearly every
Boulton and Watt cylinder for the next twenty years.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="50 in bore · ~1.5 mm error · patent 1774">
The distinction is between a tool guided by the workpiece and a tool guided by itself. A cantilever
bar is pushed sideways by the cutting force, and it is pushed hardest exactly where the metal is
thickest, so the cutter wanders away from the high spots it is meant to remove and the existing
error is copied rather than corrected. A bar held at both ends and rotating about a fixed axis
defines a straight line in space; the cut is a cylinder because the bar's motion is a cylinder,
whatever the casting was doing beforehand.

That is the general principle of a machine tool, and this is the first commercially decisive case
of it. Accuracy is being produced by the geometry of the machine rather than by the skill of the
person operating it, which means it can be repeated by someone else, on another day, to the same
figure.

The consequence for the engine is direct. Leakage past a piston depends on the gap; halve the gap
and the loss falls sharply. Watt's engines only meet their promised fuel figures once the cylinders
are bored this way, which makes this materials entry a precondition for the energy entry two stops
back on the other road.

<Forward year={1774}>
This is where a part starts being specified as a number with a permitted error rather than as a
fit judged by eye, and the idea spreads from cylinders to everything. Maudslay's screw-cutting
lathe, Whitworth's standard threads and the American system of interchangeable parts all follow
from the same premise: that accuracy is a property of the machine, and can therefore be bought,
copied and improved. The tolerance falls by roughly a factor of ten every few decades from here,
and it does not stop falling until parts are being built by depositing one atomic layer at a time
<Modern>(<Ref to="atomic-layer-deposition">atomic-layer deposition</Ref>, P.XII)</Modern>.
</Forward>

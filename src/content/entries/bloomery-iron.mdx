<Before>
Iron is not a new discovery in 1200 BCE. Meteoric iron has been worked into ceremonial objects for
a thousand years, and smiths know what the metal is. The problem is getting it out of the rock.
Iron melts at 1,538°, three hundred degrees above anything a charcoal furnace of the period can
sustain, so the copper founder's method — melt it, pour it — does not apply. Meanwhile bronze
works, and the tin arrives by ship, and there is no reason to attempt the harder metal.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The reason arrives with the collapse of the eastern Mediterranean trade system around 1200 BCE.
Tin becomes unreliable, and smiths across Anatolia and the Levant turn to a process that produces
iron without ever melting it. A bloomery — a clay shaft charged with charcoal and ore and blown
with bellows — yields a spongy lump of iron and slag called a bloom, which is then hammered hot,
repeatedly, to squeeze the slag out and consolidate the metal. The result is wrought iron. It takes
far more labour per kilogram than bronze, and for a couple of centuries it is the inferior material.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="1,538° to melt · 1,250° to reduce · never molten">
Smelting iron is a chemical problem, not a thermal one. Charcoal burning with restricted air
produces carbon monoxide, and carbon monoxide strips oxygen from iron oxide at temperatures well
below iron's melting point. What is left behind is metallic iron in solid, spongy form, mixed with
the rest of the rock. That waste fuses into a slag that melts around 1,150° and drains away, which
is why 1,250° is enough: the slag melts and the iron does not.

Wrought iron straight from a bloom is soft, because it is nearly pure. What makes it useful is
carbon: forge the iron in contact with charcoal and carbon diffuses into the surface, and quenching
that carburised layer produces steel harder than any bronze. Doing it consistently is a smith's
craft, judged by the colour of hot metal, and it will remain a matter of judgement for three
thousand years <Modern>(<Ref to="crucible-steel">crucible steel</Ref>, P.VII)</Modern>.

<Forward year="1200 BCE">
The iron age is not an upgrade, it is a substitution forced by supply, and the metal only becomes
better than bronze once smiths learn to carburise it deliberately. What changes immediately is
who can own a tool. Bronze is a currency-grade material rationed by a palace; iron ore is bog
deposits and hillside outcrops that a village can dig for itself. A ploughshare, an axe and a nail become
ordinary purchases rather than inherited capital. The furnace keeps improving in one direction,
which is hotter, until water
power pushes it past iron's melting point and the metal can finally be poured <Modern>(<Ref
to="the-blast-furnace">the blast furnace</Ref>, P.IV)</Modern>.
</Forward>

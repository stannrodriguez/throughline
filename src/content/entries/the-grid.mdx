<Before>
Electricity before 1882 is generated where it is used. A factory with an arc-lighting system owns
its dynamo, runs it off its own steam engine, and maintains it. A hotel wanting electric light buys
a plant and hires someone to look after it. Each installation carries the full capital cost of
generation, runs at whatever load it happens to have, and shuts down when the building does. The
model is the same one water power imposed in Period III: the power source is part of the premises.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Edison's Pearl Street station opens on 4 September 1882 with six large dynamos in a building on a
downtown block, feeding direct current at 110 volts through underground mains to customers within
about a square mile of it. It begins with roughly four hundred lamps across eighty-odd customers
and grows tenfold in two years. Every element is designed as part of one system — the lamp, the
mains, the fuses, the junction boxes, and the meter, without which none of it is a business.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="4 September 1882 · ~400 lamps · 110 V DC, one square mile">
The economics come from diversity of demand. No customer uses power continuously, and different
customers peak at different times, so the plant needed to supply a thousand subscribers is far
smaller than a thousand private plants. A central station also runs larger, more efficient
machines, and can bring units on and off as load changes.

The limit is the square mile, and it is a physics problem. Power lost in a conductor rises with the
square of the current, so pushing more power down a wire at low voltage wastes it as heat; Edison's
110-volt DC system cannot economically reach beyond about a kilometre. The alternative is to
transmit at high voltage and low current and step down near the customer, which requires
transformers, and transformers only work on alternating current. The resulting commercial fight —
Edison's DC against Westinghouse and Tesla's AC — is settled by the Niagara Falls installation of
1895, which sends power twenty miles to Buffalo. Every grid since is AC.

The unforgiving property of the whole arrangement is that electricity is generated and consumed in
the same instant. Supply must be matched to demand continuously, second by second, or the frequency
drifts and equipment fails.

<Forward year={1882}>
The grid becomes the largest machine ever built and the one people notice least, and it makes
electricity a commodity rather than an installation — anyone can buy any amount without owning a
generator. What it never solves is the instantaneous balance. For 130 years the system has to
follow demand exactly, which means keeping expensive plant spinning in reserve and building
generation for the worst hour of the worst day. That constraint is what makes intermittent
generation awkward, and it is the one the road's last entries take on <Modern>(<Ref
to="grid-scale-storage">grid-scale storage</Ref>, P.XII)</Modern>.
</Forward>

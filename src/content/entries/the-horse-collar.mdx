<Before>
A horse is a better draught animal than an ox on every measure except one. It moves half again as
fast, it works longer hours, and it turns at the end of a furrow more readily. The exception is
that nobody can harness it properly. The ancient throat-and-girth harness runs a strap around the
animal's neck, so that pulling hard against a load presses the strap into the windpipe and the
horse chokes itself. The animal stops not because it is out of strength but because it cannot
breathe, and the effective draught is a fraction of what the muscles could deliver.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The rigid padded collar puts the load where the ox has always carried it: on the shoulders, against
the skeleton, clear of the trachea. It appears in China several centuries earlier and comes into
general use in northern Europe by the ninth and tenth centuries, along with two supporting pieces
of equipment — the nailed horseshoe, which keeps a hoof serviceable on wet ground, and tandem
harnessing, which lets several horses pull one plough in line rather than abreast.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="3–4× draught · 50% faster than an ox · 8 hours a day">
The whole entry is a question of where a force is applied. Draught is limited by whichever comes
first: the muscle available, or the point at which the harness injures the animal. Throat-and-girth
gear reaches the second limit long before the first. A collar spreads the same pull across the
shoulder and chest, so the constraint becomes muscular, and a horse in a collar delivers three to
four times the tractive effort it could deliver in a strap.

The compound effect on farm output is larger than the ratio suggests. A horse ploughs faster than
an ox, so more land is turned inside the short window when soil is workable; it also walks to
market faster, which widens the area a farm can sell into. The catch is fodder. A working horse
needs oats, which means arable land diverted from human food, and the switch only pays where the
yield is high enough to carry it.

<Forward year="900">
This is the last big improvement in animal power, and the road spends the next eight hundred years
refining a system whose ceiling is set by how much grass and oats a country can grow. Wind and
water take the stationary work — grinding, fulling, forging — and animals keep the ploughing and
the haulage. Nothing displaces the horse from a road until an engine can be built small enough to
sit on a cart <Modern>(<Ref to="high-pressure-steam">high-pressure steam</Ref>, P.VII)</Modern>,
and the count of working horses in Britain does not peak until the twentieth century.
</Forward>

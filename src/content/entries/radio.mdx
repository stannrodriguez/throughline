<Before>
The telegraph and telephone networks are enormously capable and they end where the wire ends. A
ship at sea is unreachable from the moment it drops the pilot; so is anywhere the cost of laying
cable cannot be justified. When the Titanic's distress traffic matters eleven years after this
entry, it matters precisely because there was no other way to reach a vessel. Maxwell predicted
electromagnetic waves mathematically in 1865 and Hertz generated and detected them in the
laboratory in 1887, showing they travel at the speed of light and behave like light. Hertz saw no
practical use for them.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Guglielmo Marconi treats it as an engineering problem: bigger antennas, more power, better
detectors, and above all a properly earthed vertical aerial. He is signalling across the Channel by
1899. On 12 December 1901 an aerial held aloft by a kite at Signal Hill, Newfoundland, receives
three dots — the letter S in Morse — transmitted from Poldhu in Cornwall, about 3,500 kilometres
away. The claim is contested at the time and for good reason, since it rests on a faint signal heard
by ear at a prearranged time, but the transatlantic service that follows within a few years settles
the matter.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="12 December 1901 · ~3,500 km · the letter S">
The result should not have worked according to the physics people had. Radio waves travel in
straight lines, the Earth curves away, and Newfoundland is well below Cornwall's horizon. The
explanation, proposed independently by Heaviside and Kennelly the following year and confirmed in
the 1920s, is that a conducting layer in the upper atmosphere reflects long-wave signals back down,
so the wave bounces between ionosphere and ocean and follows the curve. Marconi succeeded partly by
not knowing this was impossible.

The deeper structure of the entry is that the signal has no dedicated path. A wire connects two
specified points and carries what is put into it; a radio transmission goes everywhere within
range, and everyone with a receiver gets it. That makes broadcasting possible, which is a genuinely
new kind of communication rather than a cheaper version of an old one, and it makes the spectrum a
shared and therefore regulated resource: two stations on one frequency interfere, so the state
allocates bands, and radio arrives already governed.

<Forward year={1901}>
Radio's first market is the one wires cannot serve — ships, then aircraft, then armies. Broadcasting
follows from 1920, and within a decade a national audience can hear the same voice at the same
moment, which is a political fact as much as a technical one. The physics also runs in the other
direction: the same equipment that sends a pulse and listens can time the echo, which is radar. What
radio never gets is capacity. Every user shares one spectrum and interferes with the others, so the
answer for high-volume traffic runs the opposite way, confining the signal so completely that
nothing escapes to interfere with anything <Modern>(<Ref to="optical-fiber">optical fiber</Ref>,
P.XI)</Modern>.
</Forward>

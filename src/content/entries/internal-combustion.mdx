<Before>
A steam engine carries its fire in one place and its working fluid in another, which means a
firebox, a boiler of water, a chimney and usually a condenser. All of that is dead weight, and it
has to be brought up to temperature before the engine will do anything — a locomotive takes hours
to raise steam. Trevithick's high-pressure engines improved the ratio of power to weight
enormously and did not change the arrangement. As long as the combustion is outside the cylinder,
the heat has to be carried across a boundary, and the boundary is heavy.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Nikolaus Otto's four-stroke engine of 1876 establishes the cycle: intake, compression, power,
exhaust, with the charge compressed before it is ignited. Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach make
it fast and light enough to be portable. Karl Benz patents the Motorwagen on 29 January 1886, a
three-wheeled vehicle with a single-cylinder engine producing about three-quarters of a horsepower
— a figure that sounds trivial and is delivered by an assembly weighing a fraction of any steam
plant that could match it. In 1888 Bertha Benz drives one 106 kilometres from Mannheim to
Pforzheim, buying fuel at a pharmacy, which is what convinces anyone the thing is usable.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="4 strokes · 0.75 hp · patent 29 January 1886">
Two things are happening. The first is the removal of the intermediate fluid: the burning gas is
itself the working gas, pushing directly on the piston, so there is no boiler, no water and no
warm-up. The second is compression. Squeezing the mixture before igniting it raises the temperature
difference the engine works across, and efficiency rises with the compression ratio — which is why
the four-stroke cycle spends a whole stroke doing nothing but compressing, and why the history of
the engine afterwards is a long argument with fuel chemistry about how hard the charge can be
squeezed before it detonates on its own.

The price is that only one stroke in four produces power, so the engine needs a flywheel to carry
it through the other three and runs unevenly at low speed. It also cannot start itself, which is
handled by a crank and later by an electric motor.

<Forward year={1886}>
The engine that carries its own fuel and needs no track is what puts vehicles on ordinary roads,
and within forty years it has rebuilt the layout of cities around it. Its second consequence is
that petroleum finds a second market at exactly the moment electric light destroys its first
one. The engine's own limit is the reciprocating motion: pistons that stop and
reverse thousands of times a minute impose a ceiling on speed and on power per kilogram, and the
next step is to let the combustion run continuously and push a wheel rather than a piston
<Modern>(<Ref to="the-gas-turbine">the gas turbine</Ref>, P.X)</Modern>.
</Forward>

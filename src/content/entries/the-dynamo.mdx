<Before>
Michael Faraday demonstrates electromagnetic induction in 1831: move a conductor through a magnetic
field and a current flows in it. For thirty years the machines built on that principle are
laboratory instruments and specialist tools — magneto-electric generators with permanent magnets,
used for electroplating and for firing blasting charges. The ceiling is the magnet. A permanent
magnet of the period is weak, and the field it provides sets the output, so making a bigger machine
means finding a bigger lump of magnetised steel. Electricity in 1860 is something you buy in
batteries by the jar.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The self-exciting dynamo removes the permanent magnet. Its field windings are electromagnets fed
from the machine's own output, so the generator builds its own field as it comes up to speed. The
process starts on the faint residual magnetism left in the iron core from the last run, which
produces a trickle of current, which strengthens the field, which produces more current. Several
people arrive at it within months in 1866 and 1867 — Werner von Siemens, Charles Wheatstone,
Samuel Alfred Varley — and Zénobe Gramme's ring armature of 1870 turns the principle into a machine
that delivers steady direct current fit for industrial use.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="1831 induction · 1867 self-excitation · residual magnetism">
Self-excitation is positive feedback held in check by saturation. More current gives more field
gives more current, which would run away except that iron can only be magnetised so far; beyond
that point extra current buys almost no extra field, and the machine settles at a stable operating
point. The engineering interest is that output now scales with how much copper and iron you are
prepared to build and how much mechanical power you can feed in, rather than with the strength of
an available magnet.

The dynamo also runs backwards. The same machine fed with current turns and becomes a motor, which
is what makes electricity worth generating: current can be produced at one place, carried along a
wire, and turned back into motion somewhere else. That is a genuinely new thing on this road. Every
previous power source — water, wind, muscle, steam — had to be located where the work was done, or
connected to it by shafts and belts running the length of a building.

<Forward year={1867}>
Motion converted to current and back to motion means that generation and use are no longer the same
place, and the consequence is that the most convenient power source can be used regardless of where
the factory is. Within fifteen years there are central stations selling the stuff by the meter
<Modern>(<Ref to="the-grid">the grid</Ref>, P.IX)</Modern>, and within thirty the same current is
being used to reduce aluminium from its ore and to melt steel without a flame <Modern>(<Ref
to="aluminum-cheap">aluminum, cheap</Ref>, P.IX)</Modern>. The dynamo is the last entry on this
road that is purely about generating power; everything after it is about what the power is for.
</Forward>

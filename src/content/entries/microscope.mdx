<Before>
The lower limit of the unaided eye is about a tenth of a millimetre. Below that is not a blur but a
blank: there is no evidence that anything is there, so nothing is assumed to be. Disease is
explained by imbalanced humours and by miasma, bad air rising from marshes and filth. Both theories
account for the observed pattern — sickness clusters where the air smells — and neither can be
tested, because the alternative hypothesis has no observable content. Fermentation, putrefaction
and infection are all processes that appear to happen on their own.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Robert Hooke, curator of experiments at the young Royal Society, publishes <em>Micrographia</em> in
1665: sixty observations made with a compound microscope, with large fold-out engravings he drew
himself. A flea, a louse, the point of a needle, the edge of a razor, and a thin slice of cork whose
regular compartments he names cells. A decade later Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a draper in Delft with
no scientific training, grinds tiny single beads of glass into mounts that reach nearly three
hundred magnifications and reports living things in pond water, in rainwater, and in the scrapings
from his own teeth.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="30× compound · 270× single lens · 1665">
Two designs, and the simpler one is the better. A compound microscope uses an objective and an
eyepiece in series, so the magnifications multiply, but so do the aberrations of both lenses;
Hooke's instrument is workable at about thirty diameters and mushy beyond it. Leeuwenhoek's is a
single almost spherical bead of glass held in a brass plate, with the specimen on a pin in front of
it. A short focal length gives high magnification directly, and one surface introduces one lens's
worth of error rather than two, so his best instruments resolve detail no compound microscope
matches for another century and a half.

The reason nothing follows medically for two hundred years is that seeing an organism is not the
same as showing it causes anything. The animalcules are in the pond and in the mouths of healthy
people; nothing in the image says which of them matter. Establishing that particular microbes cause
particular diseases requires culture on solid media, staining, and controlled inoculation — the
techniques Koch assembles in the 1870s and 1880s. The instrument arrives in 1665 and the argument
it enables arrives in 1876.

<Forward year={1665}>
<em>Micrographia</em> is a bestseller — Pepys sat up until two in the morning with it and called it
the most ingenious book he had ever read — and the thing it establishes is not any particular fact
but a habit: that the world continues below the resolution of the eye, and that the way to find out
what is there is to build a better lens. Everything on the materials road after Period IX depends
on that habit taken to its limit, since a crystal cannot be purified to one atom in a billion by
anyone who cannot see what they are removing <Modern>(<Ref to="zone-refined-silicon">zone-refined
silicon</Ref>, P.XI)</Modern>.
</Forward>

<Before>
Amplification in 1947 means a vacuum tube: a heated filament boiling electrons off into an evacuated
glass envelope, with a grid between filament and plate controlling the flow. It works, and it has
the characteristics of a light bulb because it essentially is one. It takes seconds to warm up,
consumes watts continuously to keep the filament hot, and fails regularly. ENIAC, completed in
1946, contains 17,468 of them, draws about 150 kilowatts, and spends a substantial share of its
existence being repaired — with that many components, something is nearly always broken. Scaling
any of this by a factor of a thousand is arithmetically impossible.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Bell Labs sets a group to find a solid-state amplifier, on the reasoning that the telephone
network's repeaters cannot go on being tubes. On 23 December 1947 John Bardeen and Walter Brattain
demonstrate a point-contact transistor: two gold contacts pressed very close together on a slab of
germanium, in which a small current at one contact controls a much larger one at the other.
William Shockley, irritated at having been on the outside of the discovery, works out the more
robust junction transistor over the following weeks; it is published in 1948 and manufacturable by
1951. All three share the 1956 Nobel Prize.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="23 December 1947 · ENIAC: 17,468 tubes, 150 kW · no moving part">
A semiconductor conducts poorly when pure and usefully when deliberately contaminated. Adding an
element with one more outer electron than silicon or germanium leaves spare electrons free to move;
adding one with one fewer leaves vacancies that also move. Put the two kinds of doped material
against each other and current crosses the junction easily in one direction and hardly at all in
the other. Stack three regions in sequence and a small current or voltage at the middle one governs
a large current through the whole — amplification with nothing hot, nothing evacuated and nothing
moving.

The consequence that matters is not that it replaces the tube but that it can be made small and
made together. A transistor is a pattern of impurities in a crystal, and patterns can be printed:
by 1958 Kilby and Noyce have several devices and their connections on a single piece of
semiconductor, and from there the number on a chip doubles roughly every two years for fifty years.
No device before this got cheaper as it got smaller.

<Forward year={1947}>
The transistor is the smallest object on this road and the one with the largest consequences,
because it makes switching almost free and everything digital is switching. It also inverts what
the materials road is for. Every material until now was wanted for a bulk property — strength,
hardness, melting point, elasticity. A transistor wants a crystal that is perfect except for the
imperfections put in on purpose, at a concentration of a few atoms in a billion, which is a
requirement no earlier metallurgy would have recognised as meaningful <Modern>(<Ref
to="zone-refined-silicon">zone-refined silicon</Ref>, P.XI)</Modern>.
</Forward>

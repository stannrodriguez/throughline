<Before>
Breaking ground is the limiting task in agriculture. A hoe or a digging stick turns soil at the
rate of a person’s arms and back, which caps the area one household can plant, which caps the
harvest. Heavy or root-bound soils are simply not farmed: they cost more labour to open than the
crop returns. The constraint is not knowledge of planting but the sustained power available to
open a furrow, and a working adult supplies about seventy watts of it, for a few hours, before
tiring.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The ard is a shaped wooden point dragged through the soil on a beam, with the pulling done by
cattle. It appears in Mesopotamia by roughly 4500 BCE and spreads across the Near East and Europe
over the following two millennia. It does not turn the soil over — the mouldboard that does is a
much later refinement — but it opens a furrow deep enough to sow, and it does so at the pace of a
walking animal rather than a swinging arm. By about 2300 BCE Mesopotamian ploughs carry a seed
funnel, so opening the furrow and sowing it become one pass.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="0.5 hp per ox · 0.07 hp per person · 8 : 1">
The whole entry is a ratio of sustained power. A person can hold about 0.07 horsepower, near
seventy watts, across a working day. An ox sustains roughly 0.5 horsepower, and it does so on
fodder grown on land that cannot be used for grain anyway. The ratio is somewhere between seven and
eight to one, which is the figure that matters: one animal replaces most of a work gang, and the
gang can be spent on something else.

The transfer is not free. The ox needs pasture, water and a handler, and it eats whether or not the
season needs ploughing. What makes the trade pay is that the animal converts grass — which people
cannot digest — into draught, and returns manure that raises the yield of the field it just opened.

<Forward year="4500 BCE">
The plough sets a pattern the whole energy road follows: find a source of power that is not a
person, and build the linkage that lets it do the work. For the next five thousand years the
available sources are animals, falling water and wind, and the engineering is all in the harness.
The ox is easy to yoke because it pulls from the shoulders; the horse is faster and is nearly
useless in the same gear until somebody moves the load off its windpipe <Modern>(<Ref
to="the-horse-collar">the horse collar</Ref>, P.III)</Modern>. Not until fuel itself can be made to
push will the animals come out of the field <Modern>(<Ref to="newcomen-engine">the Newcomen
engine</Ref>, P.VI)</Modern>.
</Forward>

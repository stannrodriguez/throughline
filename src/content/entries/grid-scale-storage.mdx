<Before>
Electricity has been the one commodity that must be produced at the moment it is consumed. Since
1882 grid operators have matched generation to demand continuously, which means holding plant
spinning in reserve, building capacity for the worst hour of the worst day, and paying whatever it
costs to have a gas turbine follow the evening peak. Pumped hydro has existed since the 1900s and
works well, and needs two reservoirs at different heights, which most of the world does not have.

The arrival of wind and solar makes the problem sharper rather than creating it. Output that
follows the weather instead of demand is manageable at a few percent of supply and awkward beyond
it, and a sunny afternoon in a solar-heavy grid can drive wholesale prices below zero while the
same grid struggles at seven in the evening.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Utility-scale lithium storage arrives when the cells become cheap enough, which is a consequence of
consumer electronics and electric vehicles rather than of anything the power industry did. Pack
prices fall roughly ninety percent over the decade from 2010. The installation that demonstrates it
publicly is the Hornsdale Power Reserve in South Australia — 100 megawatts and 129 megawatt-hours,
built in about a hundred days and online at the end of 2017 — which pays for a large share of
itself within two years by selling frequency control rather than energy.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="100 MW / 129 MWh · milliseconds to respond · ~90% price fall">
A grid battery is rated in two numbers that are easy to confuse: power, the rate at which it can
deliver, and energy, the total it holds. Hornsdale's 100 megawatts and 129 megawatt-hours means it
can supply at full output for a bit over an hour. Most of what these installations do is not
storage in the daily sense at all — it is frequency regulation, correcting the small continuous
mismatches between generation and load, and it is worth more per megawatt-hour than energy is.

The property that makes them good at it is response time. Grid frequency is held near 50 or 60 hertz
by matching supply to demand second by second, and a thermal plant takes minutes to change output
because it has to change the state of a large amount of hot metal. An inverter responds in
milliseconds. That is a difference of several orders of magnitude in a service the grid has always
needed and never had cheaply.

What batteries do not yet do is seasonal. Storing summer surplus for winter demand needs capacities
hundreds of times larger than anything installed, at a cost per kilowatt-hour that lithium does not
reach, which is why hydrogen, thermal stores and pumped hydro are still being argued about.

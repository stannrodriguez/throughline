<Before>
Cuneiform and Egyptian hieroglyphic both work, and both are expensive. A scribe learns several
hundred signs, their sound values, their determinatives and the conventions for combining them, and
the training runs for years in a school attached to a temple or a palace. That cost has a social
consequence: reading and writing are a salaried occupation, held by the institutions that can
afford to train practitioners, and a merchant with a cargo to record hires one rather than becoming
one.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Somewhere among Semitic-speaking workers in Egypt, around 1900 to 1500 BCE, a set of signs is
adapted by acrophony: take the Egyptian picture of a house, <em>bayt</em>, and let it stand not for
a house but for the sound it starts with. Do that a couple of dozen times and the entire sign list
collapses to one sign per consonant. This proto-Sinaitic script is refined along the Levantine
coast, and by about 1050 BCE the Phoenician form is stable: twenty-two letters, written right to
left, carried around the Mediterranean by traders. Greek borrows it a couple of centuries later and
repurposes the signs it does not need for consonants into vowels.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="22 signs · ~900 in cuneiform · 1 sign per consonant">
The economy comes from matching the notation to the size of the sound inventory rather than to the
vocabulary. A language has tens of thousands of words and a few dozen distinct consonants and
vowels. A system with one sign per word needs an inventory that grows with the dictionary; a system
with one sign per syllable needs hundreds; a system with one sign per phoneme needs a few dozen and
never needs more. Writing a word becomes a matter of hearing it rather than recalling a sign for
it, which is why the training collapses from years to weeks.

The Phoenician version records only consonants, and the reader supplies the vowels from context —
workable in a Semitic language where the consonantal root carries the meaning. Greek, where vowels
distinguish words, has to add them, and does.

<Forward year="1050 BCE">
The number that matters is not twenty-two but the training time it implies. When literacy costs
weeks, it stops being confined to the people an institution pays to have it, and starts turning up
in inventories, gravestones, curses and letters written by people with no official position at all.
The Greek and Latin descendants of these twenty-two signs are still in use three thousand years
later, and their small inventory becomes a manufacturing advantage the moment somebody tries to
build a page out of separate reusable pieces of metal <Modern>(<Ref to="movable-type">movable
type</Ref>, P.V)</Modern>.
</Forward>

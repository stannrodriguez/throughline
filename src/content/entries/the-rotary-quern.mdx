<Before>
Bread costs labour before it costs anything else. A household needs roughly a kilogram of flour per
person per day, and producing it means the saddle quern: a lower stone, an upper stone, and someone
kneeling to push the upper one back and forth across the grain. The work is slow, it is unremitting
because flour does not keep well once ground, and across the ancient world it is done by women and
slaves for several hours every day. It is very probably the single largest use of human muscle in
any settled society.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The rotary quern replaces the reciprocating stroke with a circle. A disc-shaped upper stone turns
on a spindle above a fixed lower one, grain feeds through a hole in the centre, and flour works its
way out at the rim. Hand versions spread across the Mediterranean and Europe from around the fourth
century BCE. The consequential version is larger: the Roman <em>mola asinaria</em>, an hourglass
stone turned by a donkey walking a circle, of which bakery examples survive at Pompeii. Turning is
something an animal can do all day; rocking a stone back and forth is not.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="1 kg of flour per person per day · 2 stones · continuous rotation">
The mechanical content is the conversion of the task into continuous rotary motion. A
back-and-forth stroke has to stop and reverse twice per cycle, which wastes the effort spent
accelerating the stone and cannot be done by an animal walking. Rotation never stops, so the moving
stone keeps its momentum and the power source can be anything that goes round.

Grinding itself is a shear process. The two stone faces are dressed with grooves that cut and
channel rather than crush, and the gap between them sets the fineness: wider for a coarse meal,
closer for fine flour. Because the upper stone's weight does the pressing, a heavier stone grinds
harder, and a heavier stone is exactly what an animal makes affordable.

<Forward year="300 BCE">
The quern matters less for the flour than for the shape of the machine. Once a mill is a circle
turning a shaft, whatever supplies the turning is a detail, and the next four entries on this road
are all just different things attached to the same shaft. A river will do it, continuously and for
nothing <Modern>(<Ref to="the-waterwheel">the waterwheel</Ref>, P.III)</Modern>; where no river
runs, wind will <Modern>(<Ref to="the-windmill">the windmill</Ref>, P.IV)</Modern>. The donkey is
the last power source that has to be fed.
</Forward>

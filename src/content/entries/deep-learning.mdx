<Before>
There is a class of task people perform reliably and cannot describe. Recognising a friend's face
in a crowd, telling a cat from a dog, reading handwriting, hearing a sentence through an unfamiliar
accent — everyone does these, nobody can state the rule they are following, and the reason is that
there is no compact rule to state. Fifty years of artificial intelligence tried to write them down
anyway, in expert systems and hand-crafted feature detectors, and the results were brittle: a
program that works on the examples its authors considered and fails on the next one.

Neural networks trained on examples were not a new idea either. Backpropagation was published in
1986, and Yann LeCun had convolutional networks reading handwritten digits for the postal service
by the late 1980s. They did not scale, and by the 1990s the field had largely moved on.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
In 2012 Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton enter the ImageNet Large Scale Visual
Recognition Challenge with an eight-layer convolutional network of sixty million parameters,
trained on 1.2 million photographs across a thousand categories, on two consumer graphics cards.
It finishes with a top-five error rate of 15.3 percent against 26.2 for the next best entry — a
margin far outside anything the competition had seen. The following year essentially every entrant
uses the same approach.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="26.2% → 15.3% · 60 million parameters · 1.2 million images">
The network is a stack of layers, each applying a large number of adjustable weights to its input
and passing the result through a simple non-linear function. Training compares the output to the
right answer and pushes every weight a small distance in whichever direction reduces the error,
which is calculated efficiently by the chain rule running backwards through the layers. Repeat
across millions of examples and the weights settle into something that generalises.

What the layers end up representing is the part that matters. Nobody specifies edges, textures,
shapes or ears; early layers converge on edge detectors, later ones on parts, later ones still on
objects, because those are what happen to reduce the error. The features are learned rather than
designed, which is precisely the step that hand-written systems could not take.

Nothing in this was new in 2012 except its feasibility. Three things had changed since 1986: labelled
data existed in quantities nobody had previously had, graphics processors turned out to be almost
perfectly suited to the dense matrix arithmetic involved, and a few practical adjustments —
rectified linear units, dropout — made deep stacks trainable. It is a rare case on this road of a
theory waiting decades for its inputs rather than the other way round.

<Forward year={2012}>
The methods generalise faster than anyone expects: the same architecture, scaled up and pointed at
text, audio, protein structures and game play, produces results in each. That is the claim this
entry can defend and the limit of it. A system trained on examples inherits whatever is in the
examples, cannot explain its own output any better than the person whose judgement it replaced,
and fails in ways that are hard to anticipate because nobody wrote the rules it is following.
Judgement is being automated before it is understood, and this record ends on a constraint that has
been removed without anyone being able to say exactly how.
</Forward>

<Before>
Animal skin is warm, scarce and the size the animal was. It cannot be cut to a plan without waste,
it stiffens when wet and dries, and one hide clothes one person badly. Plant fibre is abundant —
flax, hemp, nettle — but a handful of fibre is not a fabric. Twisted into cordage it makes a line;
it does not make a surface. What is missing is a way to hold thousands of short fibres in a
structure that carries load in two directions at once.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The loom holds one set of threads, the warp, straight and under tension, and passes a second, the
weft, over and under them in alternation. The warp-weighted loom does this with a row of stones
hanging from the threads; horizontal ground looms peg the tension between two beams. Textile
fragments survive from Çatalhöyük around 6000 BCE, and linen weaving is established along the Nile
by roughly 5000 BCE. The critical piece of the mechanism is the heddle: a bar that lifts alternate
warp threads together, opening a gap called the shed so the weft can be thrown through in one pass
instead of threaded over and under by hand.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="2 thread sets · warp under tension · 1 shed per pass">
Two mechanical tricks are stacked here. The first is spinning: drawing out fibres and twisting them
so that friction between the surfaces holds the bundle together under tension. A spun thread is
strong for the same reason a rope is, and neither is glued.

The second is the crossing itself. In a woven sheet every warp thread passes alternately above and
below the wefts, so each thread is held by the ones running across it. Pull the sheet in either
direction and the load distributes along a whole set of threads rather than concentrating on one.
Pull it on the diagonal and it shears and drapes, which is why cloth follows a body and a hide does
not. The structure is also open: air passes through it, so the fabric can be adjusted for a climate
by how tightly it is woven rather than by what it is made of.

<Forward year="5000 BCE">
Cloth is the first material humans design rather than find, and for the next seven thousand years
the design work is all in the arrangement — what the fibre is remains whatever grows nearby. The
loom is also a machine before there are many machines: a device with a repeating cycle, a
mechanical memory of which threads to lift, and an operator who only supplies motion. That pattern
returns when the same lifting sequence is punched into cards, and cards are what an early computing
machine reads. The fibre itself does not change until a chemist writes down a molecule that no
plant makes <Modern>(<Ref to="nylon-and-polymers">nylon and the polymers</Ref>, P.X)</Modern>.
</Forward>

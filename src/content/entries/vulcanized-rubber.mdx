<Before>
Natural rubber arrives in Europe as a curiosity and briefly becomes a business. Latex from
<em>Hevea</em> can be spread on cloth to make it waterproof, and in the 1830s there is a boom in
rubberised goods. It collapses within a few years for a simple reason: the material is unusable
outside a narrow band of temperature. In summer heat it softens, goes tacky and stinks; in winter
cold it stiffens and cracks. Warehouses of returned goods melt into unsaleable lumps. By 1839
rubber is a discredited material and the companies that made it are gone.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Charles Goodyear spends years mixing rubber with anything to hand — magnesia, quicklime, nitric
acid — and in 1839, at Woburn, drops a mixture of rubber and sulphur onto a hot stove. Instead of
melting, it chars at the edge and the rest is unchanged: firm, dry and still springy. It takes him
another five years to work out the combination of heat, time and sulphur proportion that does this
reliably, and he patents it in 1844. Thomas Hancock in England, having seen samples, files a British
patent the year before.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="−30° to 100° · a few % sulphur · patent 1844">
Raw rubber is a tangle of long polymer chains — polyisoprene — with nothing holding one chain to
the next but friction. Stretch it and the chains slide past each other, so it deforms permanently;
heat it and the sliding gets easier until the mass flows.

Sulphur heated with the rubber forms short chemical bridges between adjacent chains at intervals
along their length. The tangle becomes a single connected network. Now stretching uncoils the
chains but cannot let them slip past each other, so the material returns to its shape when released;
and heating cannot make it flow, because flow would require breaking chemical bonds rather than
merely overcoming friction. A few percent sulphur gives a soft elastic solid usable from roughly
−30° to 100°. A great deal more gives ebonite, a hard rigid plastic — the same reaction taken to
its limit.

The word vulcanisation was Hancock's colleague's, after the Roman god of fire, and it named a
process nobody could explain: the chemistry of cross-linking is not understood for another eighty
years, when Staudinger establishes that polymers are long molecules at all.

<Forward year={1839}>
Vulcanised rubber is the first engineering elastomer, and it turns out to be a component almost
every later machine needs: a seal that holds pressure while parts move, a mount that absorbs
vibration, an insulator that bends, a tyre. Goodyear spent his life in and out of debtors' prison
defending the patent and died owing money. The deeper significance takes ninety years to become
visible — this is the first time anyone improves a material by altering its molecular architecture
rather than its composition or its heat treatment, which is the whole method of the twentieth
century <Modern>(<Ref to="nylon-and-polymers">nylon and the polymers</Ref>, P.X)</Modern>.
</Forward>

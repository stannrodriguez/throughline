<Before>
Copper is the first metal worked in quantity, and it is disappointing. Native copper can be
hammered cold into ornaments and simple tools, but it is soft: an edge rolls over on the first hard
stroke, and a copper axe needs re-hammering after a morning's work. Melting it for casting means
holding 1,085°, at the very top of what a kiln can manage, and the resulting metal is still soft
when it cools. Stone remains the better material for anything that has to hold an edge.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Alloying copper with tin fixes both problems at once. Tin bronze is being made in the Near East
from around 3000 BCE, and by 1500 BCE the foundry practice is mature across the eastern
Mediterranean: about one part tin to nine of copper, melted in a crucible and poured into moulds of
stone, clay or — for anything complicated — a wax model packed in clay and then burned out. Lost-wax
casting produces a shape no smith can hammer, in one pour, and produces it repeatedly.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="1,085° copper · 950° bronze · ~10% tin">
Tin atoms are noticeably larger than copper atoms, and dropping them into the copper lattice
distorts it. Dislocations — the defects that let a metal deform — cannot glide past that distortion
easily, so the alloy resists bending and holds an edge. Hardness goes from roughly 50 on the
Vickers scale for annealed copper to 150 or more for work-hardened bronze.

The second effect is the useful one for a founder. A mixture of two metals melts lower than either
parent, so ten percent tin takes copper's 1,085° down to about 950°. The alloy is easier to melt
than the metal it is made of, it flows into fine detail before freezing, and it shrinks predictably.

The cost is entirely in logistics. Copper is common; tin, in workable ore, is not. Cornwall, the
Erzgebirge and Central Asian sources supply most of the Mediterranean Bronze Age, which means every
foundry sits at the end of a supply line thousands of kilometres long.

<Forward year="1500 BCE">
Bronze is the better metal for the next four hundred years and loses anyway. Around 1200 BCE the
palace economies of the eastern Mediterranean come apart, and with them the long-distance trade
that carried tin. A smith who cannot get tin looks at the ore in the hillside behind the village
and starts working iron instead — a harder job, at a higher temperature, for a product that is at
first inferior <Modern>(<Ref to="bloomery-iron">bloomery iron</Ref>, same period)</Modern>. That is
the pattern worth carrying forward: the material that wins is not usually the best one, it is the
one whose supply nobody can cut.
</Forward>

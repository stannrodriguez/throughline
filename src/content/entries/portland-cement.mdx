<Before>
Building in stone means quarrying, transporting and cutting blocks that must then be fitted, and
every shape has to be reachable by a mason's chisel. Lime mortar holds the blocks together and
sets by slowly reabsorbing carbon dioxide from the air, which means it does not set at all in a
sealed joint and never sets under water. Harbour works, bridge piers and anything else built in
water are therefore extremely difficult. The Romans had solved it with volcanic ash from Pozzuoli,
which reacts chemically with lime and hardens submerged, and the recipe was lost with them.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Joseph Aspdin, a Leeds bricklayer, patents in 1824 a cement made by burning finely ground limestone
with clay and grinding the product to a powder. He calls it Portland cement because the set stone
resembles Portland limestone, which is a marketing claim rather than a technical one. Aspdin's
material is fired too cool to be what the name now means; the modern high-temperature version,
burned until the charge partly fuses into hard nodules called clinker, is developed by Isaac
Charles Johnson around 1845. Marc Brunel uses Portland cement in the Thames Tunnel, and by the
1850s it is standard for harbour and sewer work.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="1,450° clinkering · patent 1824 · sets under water">
Above about 1,450° the lime and the silica and alumina in the clay stop merely sitting together and
react into new compounds — principally calcium silicates — which partly melt and fuse into clinker.
Ground fine and mixed with water, those silicates hydrate: they take water into their crystal
structure and grow interlocking needles that lock the mass together. This is a chemical reaction
with water rather than a drying, which is why concrete cures in a sealed form, cures under the sea,
and gets hotter while it does it. A large pour has to be cooled.

The consequence for building is that shape becomes cheap. A stone structure is limited to forms a
mason can cut and lift; concrete takes the shape of whatever mould can be built out of timber, at
the scale of a dam. What concrete cannot do is stretch — it carries perhaps a tenth as much tension
as compression — which is why it is a nineteenth-century material and a twentieth-century structural
system, once steel bars go inside it.

<Forward year={1824}>
Concrete becomes the most-used manufactured material on Earth by volume, and it does so by being
mediocre at everything except availability: limestone and clay are common everywhere, the
processing is one hot kiln, and the product is shipped as a powder that keeps. The pattern is
Period II's again — a material wins on supply rather than on merit. Its weakness in tension is
answered by burying steel rods where the tension will be, which only becomes affordable once steel
is a commodity <Modern>(<Ref to="bessemer-steel">Bessemer steel</Ref>, same era)</Modern>.
</Forward>

<Before>
An agricultural surplus creates an accounting problem. Grain arrives at a temple store from many
fields, is held for months, is drawn down by many people, and every one of those transactions has
to be remembered by somebody who was there. Memory is the only ledger, and memory dies, forgets and
disagrees. The partial solution in use across the Near East for four thousand years before this is
the clay token: small shaped counters, one per unit of a commodity, sealed inside a hollow clay
ball so the count cannot be altered in transit.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The sealed ball has an obvious flaw, which is that reading it destroys it. Somebody starts pressing
the tokens into the outside of the ball before sealing them in, so the surface shows what the
inside holds — and once the marks on the outside are sufficient, the tokens and the ball are
redundant. What is left is a flat tablet carrying impressed signs. The proto-cuneiform tablets from
Uruk, around 3200 BCE, are the result: a few hundred to nine hundred signs, a set of numerical
notations, and content that is almost entirely administrative. Quantities of barley, beer, textiles
and livestock, set against institutions and personal names.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="~900 signs · 3,200 BCE · clay, fired or not">
The early system is not a transcription of speech. A sign stands for a commodity or a number, not
for a sound, and the tablets can be read without knowing what language the scribe spoke. The move
that turns accounting into writing proper is the rebus: using the sign for a word to stand for its
sound in another word, the way a picture of an eye can spell the pronoun. Once signs carry sound,
anything sayable becomes writable, including names, oaths, laws and stories.

The medium is as important as the notation. River clay is free, takes a stylus impression cleanly,
and hardens on drying; a tablet that is accidentally burned in a fire becomes ceramic and lasts
indefinitely. Uruk's archives survive because the buildings holding them burned down.

<Forward year="3200 BCE">
The distance between a receipt for barley and a body of law is shorter than it looks: both are a
claim fixed in a form that outlasts the people who made it, and can be produced later against
someone who says otherwise. What the system cannot yet do is spread. Nine hundred signs means years
of training, which means writing stays inside the institutions that can afford scribes, and stays
there until the sign list is cut to a size a merchant can learn <Modern>(<Ref
to="the-alphabet">the alphabet</Ref>, same period)</Modern>. The other limit is the surface: clay
is heavy and stone is worse, and cheap writing waits on a sheet that can be made by the thousand
<Modern>(<Ref to="paper">paper</Ref>, P.III)</Modern>.
</Forward>

<Before>
A potter's kiln holds about 900°, and a well-built one with a good draught can be pushed past
1,000°. That is enough to melt copper and comfortably enough for bronze. It is not remotely enough
for iron, which stays solid until 1,538°. A furnace that could reach that temperature would need a
blast no bellows of the period can supply and a lining no clay of the period survives. On the face
of it, iron is out of reach.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The bloomery does not reach it and does not need to. It is a clay shaft a metre or two tall,
charged in alternating layers of charcoal and crushed ore, with air forced in near the base through
a nozzle. The charcoal burns with restricted oxygen, which is the whole point: incomplete
combustion produces carbon monoxide, and carbon monoxide is a reducing agent. It pulls oxygen off
the iron oxide chemically, leaving metallic iron behind as a spongy mass that never liquefies. Six
to eight hours of blowing yields a bloom, and the bloom is then hammered hot to drive out the
trapped slag.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="1,250° · 1,150° slag · 1,538° never reached">
There are two temperatures that matter and neither is iron's melting point. The first is the one at
which carbon monoxide reduces iron oxide at a useful rate, which is comfortably under 1,000°. The
second is the melting point of fayalite, the iron-silicate slag formed from the waste rock, at
about 1,150°. Run the furnace at 1,250° and the slag becomes liquid and drains to the bottom while
the iron stays solid and sticks together. The separation is done by the difference between two
melting points rather than by any filter.

Charcoal is doing two jobs here, and this is the fact that shapes the next two thousand years of
the road. It is the fuel that supplies the heat, and it is the chemical reagent that supplies the
carbon monoxide. Coal cannot substitute directly: its sulphur ruins the iron. So every furnace has
to stand within cartage distance of a forest large enough to keep burning, and the size of the iron
industry is set by the size of the woodland.

<Forward year="1200 BCE">
Everything the energy road does for the next twenty-five centuries is a refinement of the draught.
Bigger bellows make a bigger furnace; a bigger furnace holds heat better and runs hotter. Put the
bellows on a waterwheel and the temperature finally crosses 1,500°, at which point the iron melts,
runs out of the bottom of the furnace, and can be cast like bronze <Modern>(<Ref
to="the-blast-furnace">the blast furnace</Ref>, P.IV)</Modern>. The forest constraint outlives all
of it, and is only broken when somebody works out how to bake the sulphur out of coal
<Modern>(<Ref to="coke-smelted-iron">coke-smelted iron</Ref>, P.VII)</Modern>.
</Forward>

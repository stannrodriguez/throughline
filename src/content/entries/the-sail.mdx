<Before>
A boat moves at the rate its crew can paddle, and a paddler is a seventy-watt engine that tires.
Cargo makes it worse: every additional tonne of grain is another stroke, and the crew that moves
the cargo also has to be fed by it. On the Nile the geography is unforgiving in one direction —
the current runs north, so anything going upstream is muscle against water, all the way.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The oldest images of a sail are painted on Egyptian pottery of the Naqada period, around 3500 BCE:
a single square sheet set on a mast near the bow, driving a reed hull upriver. The Nile is the
ideal place for it, because the prevailing wind blows south against a current that runs north. A
boat sails up and drifts down, and neither leg needs a rower. The rig is crude, works only with the
wind roughly behind, and will not be able to sail into the wind for another three thousand years.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="~1 kW from 20 m² · 75 W per rower · wind south, current north">
The sail is a surface that converts a pressure difference into thrust. Force on a square sail rises
with the square of wind speed and directly with area, so a sheet of about twenty square metres in a
five-metre-per-second breeze feels a few hundred newtons of push. At a hull speed of two metres per
second that is on the order of a kilowatt delivered continuously, against the seventy-five watts or
so a rower sustains. One sail is worth roughly ten people, and unlike ten people it does not eat.

The limitation is direction. A square rig runs before the wind and can be worked at a broad angle,
but it cannot make progress upwind. Sailing against the wind requires a fore-and-aft rig generating
lift rather than drag, which arrives with the lateen sail in the Mediterranean and Indian Ocean
several thousand years later.

<Forward year="3500 BCE">
The sail is the longest-serving prime mover on this road. It sets the speed of trade, war and
migration for five thousand years, and the figure in the strip above does not move because of it:
a ship under sail is not faster than a horse. What the sail changes is how much can be moved and
how far, at a cost that does not scale with the crew. The same principle, mounted on a tower with
the shaft turned to a millstone, will grind grain and drain land where no river falls
<Modern>(<Ref to="the-windmill">the windmill</Ref>, P.IV)</Modern>. And the thing a sail can never
supply is a position: a captain knows the speed and not the place until a clock can be carried to
sea <Modern>(<Ref to="marine-chronometer">the marine chronometer</Ref>, P.VI)</Modern>.
</Forward>

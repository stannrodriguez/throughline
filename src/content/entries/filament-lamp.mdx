<Before>
Artificial light in 1878 is a flame. A candle gives about one candlepower and has to be trimmed; an
Argand oil lamp with a tubular wick and a glass chimney gives ten or so; gas mantles light streets
and better houses and consume oxygen, blacken ceilings and occasionally explode. Arc lighting
exists and is far too bright for a room — a carbon arc is thousands of candlepower, suited to a
lighthouse or a square, and it hisses, flickers and needs its electrodes adjusted. What nobody has
is a small, steady, safe light that can be switched on in a domestic room.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Edison's laboratory at Menlo Park demonstrates a carbon filament lamp in October 1879 that runs for
13.5 hours; carbonised bamboo filaments soon reach well over a thousand. Joseph Swan in Newcastle
has a comparable lamp at the same time and the two companies eventually merge in Britain. What is
distinctive in Edison's version is not the filament material but the electrical design: a thin,
high-resistance filament drawing a small current at a relatively high voltage, in a bulb evacuated
with a Sprengel mercury pump to a vacuum good enough that the carbon does not oxidise.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="13.5 hrs, then 1,200 · high resistance · Sprengel pump">
Incandescence is straightforward: pass current through a conductor, resistance turns the electrical
energy into heat, and above about 2,000° the material glows white. The difficulty is that anything
that hot reacts with oxygen and is gone in seconds, which makes the lamp a vacuum problem before it
is a lighting problem. Two hundred years of pump development stand behind the bulb.

The choice of a high-resistance filament is the commercial insight, and it is about copper rather
than light. Lamps have to be wired in parallel, because lamps in series all go out when one fails
and cannot be switched independently. Parallel wiring at low resistance means enormous currents in
the mains, and the cost of a distribution network is dominated by the weight of copper in it. A
high-resistance lamp draws a small current, so the same wire feeds many more of them, and the
economics of the whole system turn on that one property.

Later refinements are all about running hotter for longer: tungsten filaments from 1904, coiled to
reduce heat loss, and an inert gas fill from 1913 to suppress evaporation of the metal.

<Forward year={1879}>
Electric light is the first thing most households buy electricity for, and for about twenty years
it is the only thing — the sockets that appear later are lamp holders pressed into other service.
That matters for what follows, because a distribution network justified by lighting is then
available for motors, heating and everything else, and the utility discovers that its expensive
plant sits idle during the day. Selling daytime load to industry is what turns the lighting company
into the power company <Modern>(<Ref to="the-grid">the grid</Ref>, same era)</Modern>. The filament
itself lasts a century before it is displaced by devices that make light without making heat first.
</Forward>

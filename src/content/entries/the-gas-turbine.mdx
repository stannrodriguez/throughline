<Before>
A piston engine driving a propeller has two ceilings and both are approaching by the late 1930s.
The engine's power rises roughly with its size, and so does its weight, so the ratio improves only
slowly; and the propeller loses efficiency as its tips approach the speed of sound, which happens
well before the aircraft does. The fastest piston fighters of the period are extraordinarily
complex machines — dozens of cylinders, superchargers, intricate valve gear — extracting the last
available margin from an arrangement that has run out of room.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Frank Whittle patents a turbojet in 1930 as a young RAF officer and cannot get it funded; his first
engine runs on a test bench in 1937. Hans von Ohain, working independently in Germany with the
Heinkel company, gets to flight first: the He 178 flies on 27 August 1939. The arrangement is a
compressor, a combustion chamber and a turbine on one shaft — air is compressed, fuel is burned in
it continuously, and the hot gas turns the turbine that drives the compressor before leaving as
thrust.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="27 August 1939 · ~2/3 of turbine work to the compressor · 850° in the hot section">
The gain over a piston engine is that nothing reciprocates. A piston must be stopped and reversed
twice per revolution, and the inertial forces of doing that thousands of times a minute set the
limit on both speed and size. A turbine rotates steadily, so it can handle a far greater mass flow
of air for its weight, and it burns fuel continuously rather than in a fraction of one stroke in
four.

The arrangement is also awkwardly self-referential, which is why it took so long to build one that
worked. The turbine drives the compressor that feeds the combustion that drives the turbine, and
roughly two-thirds of the turbine's output goes straight back into compression. If the compressor
is inefficient the whole cycle produces nothing at all, and only a small deficit is needed to turn
a working engine into an expensive way to heat air. Whittle's early difficulties were mostly here.

The binding constraint is not thermodynamics but metallurgy. Efficiency rises with turbine inlet
temperature, and the blades sit in that gas continuously while spinning fast enough to tear
themselves apart. Every improvement in the sixty years afterwards is a materials improvement:
nickel superalloys, then single-crystal blades with no grain boundaries to creep along, then
internal air cooling and ceramic coatings that let a blade run in gas hotter than the alloy's own
melting point.

<Forward year={1939}>
The jet does not win the war it was built for and reshapes the peace that follows. Top speed in the
capability strip goes from 160 kilometres an hour to 900 within twenty years, and then stops:
commercial aircraft in 2020 cruise at the speed they cruised at in 1960, because past that the
drag and the fuel bill rise faster than anyone will pay for. It is the only figure in this whole
record to rise steeply and then hold for sixty years, and the reason is economic rather than
technical.
</Forward>

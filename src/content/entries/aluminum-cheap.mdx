<Before>
Aluminium is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust and one of the most expensive substances
on Earth. The reason is chemical: it bonds to oxygen so tightly that no furnace can persuade carbon
to take the oxygen away, which is the trick that works for iron, copper and lead. The only method
available in the 1850s is to displace it with sodium, itself difficult and costly to make. The
result is a metal that costs more than silver. Napoleon III is said to have reserved aluminium
cutlery for his most honoured guests, and when the Washington Monument is capped in 1884 the
capstone is a 2.85-kilogram aluminium pyramid, chosen because it is a precious metal.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
In 1886 Charles Martin Hall in Ohio and Paul Héroult in France arrive at the same process
independently, within months of each other. Both are twenty-two. Alumina will not melt at any
practical temperature — its melting point is above 2,000° — but it dissolves in molten cryolite, a
sodium-aluminium fluoride, at around 950°. Pass a heavy direct current through that solution and
aluminium collects at the cathode as a liquid pool and can be tapped. Within a few years the price
falls from roughly twelve dollars a pound to under a dollar, and then to about thirty cents.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="$12 → $0.30 a pound · 950° bath · both aged 22">
The process substitutes electrical energy for chemical persuasion. Reduction that no reducing agent
can accomplish is accomplished by forcing electrons onto the metal ions directly, and the current
required is large — a modern cell runs at hundreds of thousands of amperes at a few volts. The
consequence is that an aluminium smelter is best understood as a device for converting electricity
into metal, which is why smelters are sited at hydroelectric dams and why aluminium is sometimes
described as solid electricity.

What the metal offers in return is density. Aluminium is about a third the weight of steel for a
given volume, and alloyed with copper or magnesium it approaches structural steel's strength per
unit of weight while remaining far lighter. It also forms a thin, tenacious oxide film on exposure
to air that protects the metal underneath, so it does not rust away as iron does.

<Forward year={1886}>
A structural metal at a third the density of steel is the precondition for anything that has to
lift itself. The Wright brothers' 1903 engine has an aluminium crankcase, which is one of the
reasons it is light enough to fly; every airframe afterwards is built out of aluminium alloys, and
the jet age is as much a metallurgical event as a thermodynamic one <Modern>(<Ref
to="the-gas-turbine">the gas turbine</Ref>, P.X)</Modern>. The other lesson is about what
electricity is for. Within twenty years of Pearl Street, current is being used not to light or to
turn things but to perform chemistry no fire can perform.
</Forward>

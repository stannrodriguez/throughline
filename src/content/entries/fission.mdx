<Before>
Every energy source on this road up to here rearranges electrons. Burning coal, oil, wood or
hydrogen is chemistry — outer electrons swapping partners — and chemistry has a hard ceiling of a
few electron-volts per atom, which is why fuels cluster within a factor of two or three of each
other in energy per kilogram. Einstein's 1905 relation says that mass itself is an enormous
reservoir, and until 1938 nobody has any way to open it. Rutherford, who knew more about the
nucleus than anyone alive, called the prospect of getting useful power from it moonshine in 1933.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
In December 1938 Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann bombard uranium with neutrons and find barium
afterwards, which should be impossible — barium is roughly half uranium's mass. Lise Meitner and
Otto Frisch, working out the implication over Christmas, identify it as the nucleus splitting and
calculate the energy released. The critical additional fact is that fission also throws out spare
neutrons, so the reaction can sustain itself. On 2 December 1942, under the stands of Stagg Field
in Chicago, a lattice of graphite and uranium with cadmium control rods becomes the first
self-sustaining chain reaction. It runs at about half a watt.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="2 December 1942 · 200 MeV per fission · ~0.5 W at first criticality">
The energy comes from the nuclear binding curve. Iron sits at the bottom of it — the most tightly
bound nucleus per particle — and both heavier and lighter nuclei can move towards it and release
the difference. Splitting uranium-235 into two mid-sized fragments releases about 200 million
electron-volts, against a few electron-volts for burning a carbon atom, and that ratio of roughly
two hundred million to one is the entire entry.

Sustaining it is a counting problem. Each fission emits two or three neutrons; the reaction
continues if on average exactly one of them causes another fission. Below one it dies, above one it
grows exponentially. What makes a reactor controllable rather than explosive is that a small
fraction of those neutrons are delayed, emitted seconds after the fission rather than instantly, so
a reactor held just barely critical responds on a timescale of seconds and a person with a control
rod can keep up with it.

The engineering afterwards is almost all about the neutrons and the waste. Fast neutrons must be
slowed by a moderator to be captured efficiently; the fission fragments are intensely radioactive
and stay so for a long time; and the same physics that yields power yields a weapon, which is the
context every reactor is built in.

<Forward year={1942}>
Fission changes the ceiling and not the plumbing. A reactor makes heat, the heat boils water, the
steam turns a turbine — the same arrangement Watt would recognise, with a different fire. What is
genuinely new is a fuel whose supply is effectively unconstrained by extraction, which for the
first time makes the energy-per-person figure a question of what a society chooses to build rather
than of what it can dig up. Whether that promise is kept is a matter of cost, waste and public
consent rather than physics, and the strip's last two periods show the figure levelling off
regardless.
</Forward>

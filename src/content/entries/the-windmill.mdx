<Before>
The watermill is the best machine of the age and it cannot be built most places. It needs a stream
with enough fall to drive a wheel, a site to dam, and a legal right to the water, and in flat
country none of those exist. East Anglia, the Low Countries, Denmark and the north European plain
grind their grain by hand or by animal, and pay a mill-owner upstream for anything more. A quarter
of Europe is doing the work of Period II because of its topography.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The European windmill appears in the last quarter of the twelfth century — the earliest firm record
is a mill at Weedley in Yorkshire, leased in 1185 — and it solves the direction problem in the most
direct way available. The whole mill, sails and gearing and millstones together, sits on a massive
upright post and is pushed around bodily on a tailpole until the sails face the wind. Persian
windmills of the ninth century had used a vertical axis inside a walled enclosure, which needs no
turning and catches far less wind; the European horizontal-axis form takes the harder mechanical
problem in exchange for the better one.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="2–8 hp · 1185 first record · whole body turns">
A windmill sail is a wing, not a scoop. It is set at an angle to the wind so that air passing over
it generates lift, and it is the lift component that turns the shaft — which is why the sails are
twisted along their length, more steeply pitched near the hub where they move slowly and flatter at
the tips where they move fast. Power available rises with the cube of wind speed, so a mill that
gives two horsepower in a light breeze gives sixteen in a wind twice as strong. The miller's real
skill is furling canvas fast enough to stop the machine tearing itself apart.

The corresponding weakness is that wind is not there when it is not there. A watermill runs
whenever the stream runs, which in a decent catchment is most of the year; a windmill runs when the
weather says so, and mills grain at three in the morning if that is when the wind arrives. Every
windmill country ends up with a milling trade organised around waiting.

<Forward year="1185">
The windmill puts a few horsepower wherever the wind blows, which turns out to matter most for
water rather than grain. From the fifteenth century Dutch mills are put to lifting water out of
low ground with scoop wheels and Archimedes screws, and the polder — land held below sea level by
continuous pumping — becomes possible on a national scale. That is the high-water mark of this
road's first half: every power source available to 1700 is wind, water or muscle, and every one of
them has to be built where the wind, the water or the fodder already is. The next entry on this
road is the first that can be built anywhere <Modern>(<Ref to="newcomen-engine">the Newcomen
engine</Ref>, P.VI)</Modern>.
</Forward>

<Before>
Fusion is what the Sun does and it is not obviously hard: press light nuclei together until they
join, and the products weigh slightly less than the ingredients, with the difference released as
energy. Per kilogram it beats fission, and the fuel is hydrogen isotopes, one of which comes out of
seawater. It has also been twenty or thirty years away since roughly 1955. The reason is that
nuclei are positively charged and repel each other fiercely, so joining them requires temperatures
around a hundred million degrees — several times the centre of the Sun, which compensates with a
gravitational pressure no laboratory can reproduce. Nothing solid can contain a fuel that hot, so
it must be held by magnetic fields or by its own inertia for the instant of the reaction.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The National Ignition Facility takes the inertial route. One hundred and ninety-two laser beams are
fired simultaneously into a small gold cylinder, which floods with X-rays and implodes a
peppercorn-sized capsule of deuterium and tritium to a density many times that of lead. On 5
December 2022 a shot delivers 2.05 megajoules to the target and the resulting reaction releases
3.15 — the first time a fusion reaction anywhere has produced more energy than was delivered to the
fuel. The burn lasts a few nanoseconds. The facility drew roughly 300 megajoules from the grid to
fire the lasers.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="2.05 MJ in, 3.15 MJ out · ~300 MJ from the wall · 100 million °">
The condition to satisfy is a product rather than a single quantity: density, temperature and
confinement time multiplied together must exceed a threshold. There are two ways to reach it.
Magnetic confinement — the tokamak, and ITER under construction in France — holds a thin plasma in
a magnetic bottle for seconds; JET in Britain sustained 59 megajoules over five seconds in 2022.
Inertial confinement goes the other way, compressing a tiny mass to enormous density and relying on
its own inertia to hold it together for the nanoseconds the reaction needs.

Which number counts as gain is where care is required, and the entry's dek says the honest version.
The 3.15 against 2.05 is target gain, measured at the capsule. Counting the electricity that went
into the lasers, the shot returned about one percent of what it consumed. Counting the plant that
would be needed to turn the neutrons into electricity would reduce it further. The result is a
genuine scientific threshold crossed for the first time and not an energy source.

The engineering beyond it is untouched. A power station would need shots several times a second
rather than one a day, a way to breed its own tritium, and materials that survive a neutron flux
that makes them brittle and radioactive.

<Forward year={2022}>
The road ends where it began, on the problem of holding a reaction at a temperature long enough for
it to pay. Fire took a few hundred thousand years to become routine, the bloomery took the
temperature to 1,250°, the blast furnace to 1,500°, the arc furnace to 3,500°, and this asks for a
hundred million. Whether the last step arrives is not something this record can say. What it can
say is that the previous eleven periods each removed a constraint that had held for centuries, and
that in every case the people involved were solving a specific and rather practical problem at the
time.
</Forward>

<Before>
Every book in Europe is written out by hand, one at a time. A trained scribe manages two or three
pages a day, so a Bible is a project of a year or more and costs accordingly — a bound volume is
worth a small farm. The economics follow: books exist mainly in monasteries and universities,
titles survive only where somebody chose to copy them again, and every copy introduces new errors
that the next copy inherits. Woodblock printing exists and works, but a block is one page carved
whole, useful for a page that will be reprinted for years and useless for anything else.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Johannes Gutenberg, a Mainz goldsmith, assembles four things between about 1440 and 1450: a hand
mould that casts a letter of exactly the right height and body in a few seconds, an alloy that
takes the cast cleanly, an oil-based ink that sticks to metal instead of beading off it, and a
screw press adapted from the wine trade. Movable type is not itself new — Bi Sheng made ceramic
type around 1040 and Korean foundries were printing with cast metal type by 1377 — but the
combination of the hand mould with an alphabet of twenty-six letters is what makes the process
economic. The 42-line Bible of about 1455 runs to roughly 180 copies.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="~180 copies · 250 sheets/hr · 20 million books by 1500">
The mould is the machine. It is a two-part hinged box holding a matrix — a copper blank struck with
a steel punch of the letter — into which molten alloy is poured; open the mould, tip out the type,
close it, repeat. Every piece leaves it at identical height so the page prints evenly, and one
punch-cutter's work supports an unlimited number of casts. Setting a page then becomes assembly
rather than carving, and the page can be broken up afterwards and the metal reused.

The arithmetic that follows is the entry. Composing a page takes a compositor a few hours, which is
a fixed cost paid once. Each subsequent impression takes seconds. A scribe's cost per copy is flat,
so a hundred copies cost a hundred times one; a printer's cost per copy falls with the run, so the
second hundred is nearly free. That is the first time in the history of information that making
more of something is cheaper per unit than making less.

<Forward year={1450}>
Fifty years after Mainz there are printing houses in more than two hundred European towns and
something like twenty million volumes in circulation. What that does is less about any single book
than about what becomes checkable: two people in different cities can now consult copies that are
identical page for page, and disagree about the content rather than about what the text says. The
number that does not move is the one in the strip above. A printed page still travels at the speed
of a horse, and it will keep doing so for another four hundred years <Modern>(<Ref
to="electric-telegraph">the electric telegraph</Ref>, P.VIII)</Modern>. More copies, carried no
faster.
</Forward>

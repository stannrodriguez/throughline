<Before>
Every furnace in this history has been a fire, and a fire brings its combustion products with it.
Carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, sulphur from the fuel and nitrogen from the air are all in contact
with the metal, and they take part in the chemistry whether or not the metallurgist wants them to.
That is tolerable when the aim is to reduce ore, where the carbon is doing useful work, and it is a
serious obstacle when the aim is a steel of exactly specified composition. There is also a ceiling:
the hottest flame available, with the best preheating, is around 1,900°, which limits what can be
melted at all.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Paul Héroult — the same engineer as the aluminium cell — builds a commercial electric-arc steel
furnace at La Praz in Savoy in 1900. Graphite electrodes are lowered towards a charge of scrap
steel until current arcs across the gap, and the arc itself, a column of ionised gas, sits at
several thousand degrees. There is no fuel and no flame. The atmosphere inside the furnace is
whatever the operator puts there, and the heat is delivered by a current that can be turned up or
down like a tap.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="3,500° · 1900 · no combustion products">
An arc is not a flame. Current passing through ionised gas dissipates energy in a very small
volume, and the resulting temperature is set by electrical power and geometry rather than by the
energy released in a chemical reaction. That removes the thermodynamic ceiling that combustion
imposes and puts the arc around 3,500°, which is above the melting point of essentially everything
a metallurgist wants to melt.

The consequence that matters more is compositional control. Alloying elements like chromium
oxidise readily, so in a fuel-fired furnace they burn away into the slag before they can do their
job — which is why stainless steel is not a nineteenth-century material. In an arc furnace the
atmosphere can be kept neutral, so chromium, nickel, tungsten and vanadium survive to be part of the
alloy. The tool steels and stainless steels of the twentieth century are all products of this
furnace.

It also runs on scrap. A charge of used steel and a supply of electricity are all a mini-mill needs,
which is why steelmaking is no longer tied to ore fields the way the blast furnace made it.

<Forward year={1900}>
This is the entry where the materials road's own yardstick runs out. Temperature had been the gate
for eight thousand years — 900° for pottery, 1,250° for iron, 1,500° for cast iron, 1,600° for
crucible steel — and past 3,500° there is very little left worth melting. The interesting question
becomes what is in the material rather than how hot you can get it, and every entry after this one
is measured in purity or in molecular arrangement rather than in degrees <Modern>(<Ref
to="zone-refined-silicon">zone-refined silicon</Ref>, P.XI)</Modern>.
</Forward>

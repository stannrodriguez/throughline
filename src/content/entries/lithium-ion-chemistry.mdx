<Before>
A conventional battery works by consuming its electrodes. Lead-acid converts lead and lead dioxide
into lead sulphate and back; zinc-carbon dissolves the zinc case. Each cycle physically rebuilds
the electrode's structure, and the rebuild is never perfect — crystals coarsen, material sheds,
capacity fades. A rechargeable cell of this kind is a controlled erosion with a limited number of
repetitions in it.

Lithium metal would be the ideal electrode on every count except that one. Recharging plates the
metal back unevenly, and the deposits grow as dendrites — needles that extend across the cell until
they touch the other electrode, short it, and ignite the electrolyte. Several companies shipped
lithium-metal rechargeables in the 1980s and recalled them.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Intercalation avoids the problem by never forming metal at all. John Goodenough's group showed in
1980 that lithium cobalt oxide releases lithium ions reversibly while its layered structure stays
intact; Rachid Yazami and others established that graphite will accept those ions between its
sheets. Akira Yoshino combined the two into a working cell in 1985, and Sony brought it to market
in 1991. Lithium exists in the cell only as ions shuttling between two hosts, which is why the
arrangement was nicknamed the rocking-chair battery.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="Sony, 1991 · lattice change under 10% · hundreds of cycles">
Both electrodes are layered crystals with space between the layers, and the lithium ions sit in
that space as guests. Charging drives them out of the cobalt oxide, through the electrolyte, and in
between the graphite sheets; discharging reverses it. The host lattices expand and contract by
under ten percent as the guests come and go and are otherwise unchanged, so there is no dissolution,
no replating and no structural rebuild to go wrong. That is what buys the cycle life.

The failure modes that remain are all about the hosts rather than the ions. Repeated expansion
eventually cracks particles and breaks their electrical contact; a surface film grows on the anode
that consumes a little lithium each cycle; and holding the cell full or hot accelerates both. A
battery ages by the slow degradation of its architecture, which is why capacity fades gradually
rather than failing outright.

Silicon anodes, which would hold roughly ten times as much lithium as graphite, run into exactly
this constraint: silicon swells by around three hundred percent when it takes lithium in, and
pulverises itself within a few cycles unless the structure is engineered to accommodate it.

<Forward year={1991}>
Intercalation is a general principle rather than a single chemistry, and the thirty years since have
mostly been substitutions within it — iron phosphate for cobalt, where cost and safety matter more
than density; nickel-manganese blends for vehicles. It also states the materials road's final
position plainly. The interesting property of a material here is not its strength, its melting point
or even its purity, but the shape of the space inside it, and whether that space survives being
repeatedly filled and emptied <Modern>(<Ref to="atomic-layer-deposition">atomic-layer
deposition</Ref>, same era)</Modern>.
</Forward>

<Before>
The Newcomen engine has been pumping mines for fifty years and burns coal at a rate nobody outside
a colliery can justify. The reason is structural. Steam fills the cylinder, cold water is sprayed
in to condense it, and the vacuum that forms pulls the piston down — but the same spray that
condenses the steam also chills the iron walls, and the next charge of steam has to reheat the
whole cylinder before it can fill it. Every stroke pays twice: once for the work, once for undoing
the cooling. Under one percent of the coal's energy comes out as work.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
In 1765 Watt, employed to maintain instruments at the University of Glasgow, is repairing a model
Newcomen engine and finds it consumes an absurd amount of steam for its size. His answer is to
separate the two temperatures that Newcomen's design forces into one vessel: run a pipe from the
cylinder to a second chamber kept permanently cold, and condense there. The cylinder can then stay
hot all the time — lagged, and jacketed with steam. He patents it in 1769, spends years failing to
build one that seals, and goes into partnership with Matthew Boulton in 1775.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="75% less coal · <1% → ~3% · patent 1769">
A heat engine's efficiency depends on the temperature difference it works across, and a cylinder
that swings between hot and cold every stroke spends most of its fuel moving its own walls up and
down that range. Splitting the machine so that one part is always hot and the other always cold
removes that loss entirely. The measured result is a fuel saving of roughly three-quarters for the
same work — from well under one percent of the coal's energy converted to something nearer three.

Boulton and Watt sell the saving rather than the machine: customers pay an annual premium of a
third of the value of the coal the engine saves against a Newcomen doing the same job, which
requires metering, and produces some of the first industrial performance data anywhere.

Two later additions matter as much as the condenser. The sun-and-planet gear of 1781 converts the
beam's rocking into rotation, which turns a pump into a source of power for machinery of any kind.
The centrifugal governor of 1788 uses the engine's own speed to throttle its own steam — a machine
correcting itself from its own output, and one of the first feedback controllers built for a
commercial purpose.

<Forward year={1769}>
The rotative engine is what takes the mill off the river. A Newcomen engine pumps, and pumping is
all it does; an engine that turns a shaft can drive whatever a waterwheel used to drive, and can be
built in a town, next to a coalfield, at a size chosen by the owner rather than by the stream. That
is the end of the geographic constraint the energy road has carried since Period III. Watt's own
patent, extended to 1800, becomes the next constraint — he sues to suppress high-pressure designs
he considers unsafe, and the engine that can move itself waits for the patent to lapse
<Modern>(<Ref to="high-pressure-steam">high-pressure steam</Ref>, two years later)</Modern>.
</Forward>

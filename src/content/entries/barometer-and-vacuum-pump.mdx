<Before>
Two facts sit unexplained in 1640. Aristotelian physics holds that a vacuum cannot exist, and every
observation appears to support it: pull a piston back and water follows it up the cylinder, as
though the water were being drawn by nature's refusal to permit a void. And the well-diggers of
Florence report a stubborn practical limit — a suction pump will not lift water more than about ten
metres, no matter how well made. If nature abhors a vacuum, it is unclear why it should stop
abhorring it at ten metres.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Evangelista Torricelli, working in Florence in 1643, uses mercury instead of water because it is
about fourteen times denser and the apparatus can therefore sit on a bench. He fills a metre-long
glass tube sealed at one end, inverts it into a dish, and the column falls to about 760 millimetres
and stops. Nothing is holding it up from above, and the space that opens at the closed end contains
nothing that was not already there. In 1648 Pascal has the experiment carried up the Puy-de-Dôme,
where the column stands shorter — because there is less air above it. In 1654 Otto von Guericke
pumps the air out of two joined copper hemispheres at Magdeburg and has teams of horses fail to
pull them apart.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="760 mm mercury · 10.3 m of water · 16 horses at Magdeburg">
The explanation reverses the question. Nothing pulls the column up; the atmosphere pushes it up,
pressing down on the mercury in the dish with the weight of the air standing above it. The column
rises until its own weight balances that push, which for mercury is 760 millimetres and for water,
about fourteen times less dense, is 10.3 metres. That is the well-diggers' limit, and it is a
property of the air rather than a property of the pump.

Two things follow that matter more than the measurement. The first is that the pressure is not
trivial: roughly ten newtons on every square centimetre, which across an ordinary door is several
tonnes, unnoticed because it presses equally on both sides. Remove it from one side, as von Guericke
did at Magdeburg, and sixteen horses cannot overcome it. The second is that the height of the column
varies — with altitude, and from day to day with the weather — which turns the apparatus into an
instrument. The barometer is the first device to measure something nobody had known was there.

<Forward year={1643}>
This entry sits on the information road because its product is a number, and the number is what
makes the next century possible. Boyle and Hooke build a working air pump in 1659 and get a
relation between pressure and volume out of it; the pump becomes standard laboratory equipment.
But the consequence that bends the energy line is cruder than any of that. If the atmosphere
presses with several tonnes across an ordinary area, and if a vacuum can be produced on demand,
then a cylinder emptied of air is a machine waiting for a piston — and condensing steam is a cheap
way to empty one <Modern>(<Ref to="newcomen-engine">the Newcomen engine</Ref>, sixty-nine years
on)</Modern>.
</Forward>

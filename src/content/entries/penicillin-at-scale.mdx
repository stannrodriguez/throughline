<Before>
Bacterial infection is the ordinary way people die. A scratch can be fatal; pneumonia kills a third
of the people who get it; surgery is dangerous mainly because of what happens afterwards.
Alexander Fleming notices in 1928 that a stray <em>Penicillium</em> mould has killed the
staphylococci around it on a culture plate, publishes, and does not pursue it, largely because the
active substance is present in tiny quantities and falls apart during purification. It sits in the
literature for twelve years.

Howard Florey and Ernst Chain at Oxford take it up in 1939 and prove it works: infected mice
survive, and in 1941 a policeman with septicaemia improves dramatically. Then the supply runs out —
they had been recovering the drug from his urine to re-use it — and he dies. The chemistry is
solved. The manufacturing is not.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The Oxford team takes the problem to the United States in 1941, and it is handed to the Northern
Regional Research Laboratory in Peoria, a station set up to find uses for agricultural surpluses.
Three things come out of it. Corn-steep liquor, a waste product of the local starch industry,
raises yields roughly tenfold as a growth medium. A better mould is found — after a worldwide
search, on a cantaloupe from a Peoria market — and improved further by irradiating it and selecting
the most productive mutants. And the growth is moved out of shallow flasks into deep steel tanks
with sterile air blown through and stirrers keeping the culture mixed. Output reaches 21 billion
units in 1943 and 6.8 trillion in 1945.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="21 billion units in 1943 · 6.8 trillion in 1945 · $20 → $0.55 a dose">
Penicillin interferes with the construction of the bacterial cell wall. Growing bacteria continually
build and cross-link a peptidoglycan mesh that holds them against their own internal pressure; the
drug blocks the enzyme that makes the cross-links, so a dividing cell fails structurally. Human
cells have no such wall, which is why the compound is close to harmless to the patient — an
unusually clean case of a target present in the pathogen and absent in the host.

The manufacturing problem is that the mould grows on a surface, and surface area scales badly. The
switch to submerged culture in stirred, aerated tanks is what changes the economics, and it is
mostly an oxygen-transfer problem: keeping millions of litres of broth uniformly supplied with air
and free of every competing organism. That plant is the origin of industrial biotechnology, and
insulin, vaccines and monoclonal antibodies are all made in its descendants.

<Forward year={1943}>
The instrument that first showed the organisms was built in 1665, the argument that they cause
disease was settled in the 1870s, and the manufacturing that made treatment ordinary arrives in
1943. Each step waited on the one before it and on nothing else. What follows is a change in the
shape of a life: infectious disease drops out of the leading causes of death in industrial
countries within a generation, and the diseases that remain are the ones that come with living
longer. The counter-pressure is built into the mechanism, since any organism that survives the drug
is selected for, and resistance appears in the clinic within a few years of the first mass use.
</Forward>

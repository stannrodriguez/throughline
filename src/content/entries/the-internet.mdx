<Before>
Every communication network to this point works by circuit switching. Making a telephone call means
building a physical path from one handset to the other and reserving it for the duration, whether or
not anyone is speaking; a call that uses a tenth of the capacity ties up all of it. That is an
efficient design for continuous speech and a terrible one for computers, which send data in short
bursts separated by long silences. It is also fragile in a way that matters to the people funding
this work in the 1960s: switching centres are single points of failure, and a network built around
a hierarchy of them can be disconnected by destroying a few.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Paul Baran at RAND, working on survivable communications, and Donald Davies at the National
Physical Laboratory, working on computer response times, independently arrive at the same idea in
the mid-1960s: chop each message into small blocks, address each one, and let them travel
separately through a mesh in which every node forwards towards the destination. Davies calls them
packets. ARPANET is built on the principle and its first message is sent on 29 October 1969 from
UCLA to Stanford Research Institute. The intended word was LOGIN; the receiving machine crashed
after the O. On 1 January 1983 the network switches to TCP/IP, which is the protocol still in use.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="29 October 1969 · two letters, then a crash · TCP/IP on 1 January 1983">
Packets change what a network guarantees. Nothing is reserved and no path is fixed, so capacity is
shared statistically: many bursty senders fit down one link because their silences interleave. Each
packet carries its own destination, so a router only needs to know which neighbour is generally
towards where it is going, and if a link fails the next packet takes a different route with nobody
being told.

The design decision that gave the system its longevity is the end-to-end principle. The network is
deliberately made unreliable and stupid — it may drop packets, duplicate them or deliver them out
of order — and the work of noticing and correcting all of that is put in the machines at the two
ends, in TCP. The consequence is that the middle of the network needs to know nothing about what is
being carried, so a new application requires no permission from anyone who owns a wire. The web,
video, telephony and everything since ran over an unchanged network because of it.

<Forward year={1969}>
For twenty years this is infrastructure for research institutions and nothing else, and the thing
that turns it into a public medium is a document format layered on top in 1989. The deeper
consequence is the one the design was not aiming at: because the network is indifferent to content,
anything that can be represented as data ends up travelling on it, and industries built on
controlling distribution — post, music, television, newspapers, telephony — find their distribution
is now free for anyone. It also collects, incidentally, the largest body of recorded human output

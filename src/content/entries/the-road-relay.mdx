<Before>
A message travels at the speed of the person carrying it. A runner covers perhaps forty kilometres
in a day and then needs to rest; a rider on one horse manages more, but the horse tires faster than
the rider does, and a hard-pressed horse is ruined. For an empire, this sets a hard limit on
government: an order takes as long to reach a province as a person takes to get there, and a report
of trouble takes just as long to come back. Susa to Sardis on foot is about ninety days.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The Persian relay separates the message from the messenger. Darius I's royal road runs roughly
2,700 kilometres from Susa to Sardis, with staging posts a day's ride apart, each holding fresh
horses and a rider waiting. A dispatch is handed on at every post and keeps moving while horses and
riders rest. Herodotus reports the crossing in seven to nine days, which is a tenfold improvement
on walking it. Rome builds the same system as the <em>cursus publicus</em>, and China, the Mongol
empire and the Ottomans all build versions of it.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="2,700 km · 7 days · 200 km/day">
The insight is that the constraint is the animal's endurance, not its speed. A horse at a fast
working pace covers fifteen kilometres an hour, but cannot keep it up for more than a couple of
hours. Chain enough rested horses together and the message inherits the sprint speed of each one
without ever inheriting the fatigue, so the daily figure lands at roughly two hundred kilometres
instead of the sixty or so one horse and rider could sustain.

Everything else in the system exists to protect that number. The road is surfaced and patrolled,
because a message that has to pick its way is slower than one that does not. The posts are
provisioned by the state, because a rider who has to negotiate for a horse has already lost an
hour. What the system cannot do is scale down: the relay is worth building for an empire's
dispatches and for nothing else, so ordinary correspondence still moves at the speed of whoever is
walking that way.

<Forward year="500 BCE">
Two hundred kilometres a day is the fastest information moves for the next twenty-three centuries.
Rome does not beat it, the Caliphates do not beat it, the Mongols match it, and the messenger
carrying news of Waterloo in 1815 travels no faster than the one carrying news of Marathon. The
number survives the invention of printing intact, because a press makes more copies of a message
without making any of them travel quicker <Modern>(<Ref to="movable-type">movable type</Ref>,
P.V)</Modern>. It survives until a signal can be sent without anything physical making the journey
<Modern>(<Ref to="electric-telegraph">the electric telegraph</Ref>, P.VIII)</Modern>.
</Forward>

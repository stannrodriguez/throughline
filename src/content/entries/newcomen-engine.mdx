<Before>
England has burned through its forests and runs on coal, but the easy coal is gone. Mines now chase
seams below the water table, and they flood. The state of the art is a gin: a circle of horses
walking all day to lift buckets of water, eating the profits of mines that exist to feed furnaces.
Savery's steam pump exists, but it can only raise water a few meters, and it tends to explode.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Thomas Newcomen, an ironmonger who sells tools to miners, combines the piston with condensation:
fill a cylinder with steam, spray in cold water, and let the atmosphere itself do the work. His
engine at the Conygree coalworks runs twelve strokes a minute and lifts water from fifty meters
down — the first machine in history that burns fuel to produce continuous mechanical work.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="1,600 : 1 · 22 kN · <1%">
Steam at atmospheric pressure occupies about 1,600 times the volume of the water that made it — so
when the cold spray condenses it, the cylinder's pressure collapses toward a vacuum. The engine's
power comes not from steam pushing but from the ordinary atmosphere pressing the piston down into
that void: about ten newtons on every square centimeter, which across a half-meter piston is
roughly two tonnes of force, delivered by air.

The efficiency is dreadful. Under one percent of the coal's energy becomes work, because the cold
spray also chills the cylinder walls, which the next charge of steam must reheat. That waste is
invisible at a pithead where coal is nearly free — and every stroke, the engine reheats a cylinder
it just chilled, a fault so obvious in hindsight that it will take sixty years for anyone to see
it.

<Forward year={1712}>
The engine at Conygree wastes ninety-nine parts of coal in a hundred, and nobody standing at the
pithead can be made to care — coal is the one thing a colliery has. But a Scottish
instrument-maker, not yet born, will care very much: he will notice the chilled cylinder, move the
condensation elsewhere, and cut the coal bill by three-quarters <Modern>(<Ref
to="watt-separate-condenser">Watt's separate condenser</Ref>, next stop, sixty years
hence)</Modern>. And once fuel makes motion cheaply, motion will stop caring where the rivers are:
the mills will leave the hillsides for the towns <Modern>(<Ref to="high-pressure-steam">steam small
enough to move itself</Ref>, P.VII)</Modern>, and the wagons will leave the horses behind
<Modern>(<Ref to="the-dynamo">the dynamo</Ref>, P.VIII, when motion learns to travel by
wire)</Modern>.
</Forward>

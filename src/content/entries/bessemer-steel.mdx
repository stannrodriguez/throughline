<Before>
Three iron products exist and none of them is both good and cheap. Cast iron from the blast furnace
is abundant, hard and brittle. Wrought iron, made by puddling — a workman stirring a bath of molten
pig iron with a rod until the carbon burns off — is tough, expensive in labour, and produced in
lumps of a hundredweight or so at a time. Crucible steel is excellent and comes twenty kilograms to
the pot. Railways are being built out of wrought iron rails that wear out in a couple of years under
traffic, because steel at fifty pounds a ton is out of the question for anything laid by the mile.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Henry Bessemer's converter, patented in 1856, is a tilting vessel that holds several tonnes of
molten pig iron with tuyères in the bottom. Cold air is blown up through the melt. The carbon and
silicon dissolved in the iron oxidise, and because those reactions are strongly exothermic the
charge gets hotter as it is refined — the process needs no fuel under the vessel at all. A blow
lasts about twenty minutes and finishes with a spectacular flame at the mouth. Bessemer's first
licensees fail badly, because the iron they use is high in phosphorus and the product cracks; the
Thomas-Gilchrist basic lining of 1878 fixes that by lining the vessel with a material that takes
phosphorus into the slag.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="20 t in 20 min · £50 → £7 a ton · no external fuel">
The insight is that the impurity is the fuel. Pig iron carries three to four percent carbon, and
burning that carbon releases enough heat to raise the whole charge above steel's melting point,
which is higher than the pig iron's was. The bath therefore stays liquid as it is purified, when
intuition says it should freeze.

The difficulty is stopping. Blow too long and every trace of carbon is gone, leaving soft, brittle
burnt iron rather than steel. Bessemer's own answer was to guess at the flame; Robert Mushet's,
which is what made the process usable, is to blow the carbon out completely and then add back a
weighed quantity of spiegeleisen, an iron-manganese-carbon alloy, to bring the composition to where
it should be. Composition becomes a recipe again, as it did in the crucible, but now at fifty
times the scale.

<Forward year={1856}>
Cheap steel is the material fact of the second half of the nineteenth century. Rails last years
instead of months, which is what makes heavy freight economic; ship plate gets thinner and stronger,
which is what makes steam hulls economic; and steel framing, with cement and reinforcement, is what
makes a building taller than masonry allows. The process itself is superseded within a lifetime by
open-hearth and then oxygen steelmaking, but the threshold it crosses is not crossed again: after
1856 steel is a commodity, and the interesting question about a material moves from whether it can
be made in quantity to how precisely its composition can be controlled <Modern>(<Ref
to="electric-arc-furnace">the electric-arc furnace</Ref>, P.IX)</Modern>.
</Forward>

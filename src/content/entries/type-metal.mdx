<Before>
A printing type has to satisfy four requirements that pull against each other. It must melt at a
temperature low enough to be poured into a small hand-held mould and set within seconds, or a
compositor cannot cast the tens of thousands of pieces a book needs. It must be hard enough to
survive thousands of impressions under pressure without the face rounding off. It must reproduce
the punch-cutter's letterform exactly, including the fine serifs. And it must be cheap and
remeltable, because the metal is tied up in standing pages and has to be recovered.

Lead melts low, casts easily and is far too soft. Tin is harder and expensive. Copper and bronze
hold detail and an edge and melt far too high for a hand mould. No single metal does the job.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The solution is a ternary alloy: mostly lead, with tin and antimony added. Analyses of surviving
fifteenth-century type give something near 83 percent lead, 9 percent tin and 6 percent antimony,
with traces of copper. It melts at around 300°, well within reach of a small furnace, and it is
hard enough to print long runs. The property that makes it work is peculiar to antimony, and it is
the reason this entry sits on the materials road rather than being a footnote to the press.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="~300° melt · 83% lead, 9% tin, 6% antimony · thousands of impressions">
Almost every metal contracts as it solidifies, because the atoms pack more tightly in a crystal
than they do in a liquid. For a founder casting a bell that is a nuisance to be allowed for; for a
founder casting a letter two millimetres across it is fatal, because the metal pulls away from the
mould exactly where the detail is finest and the serifs come out soft or missing.

Antimony is one of the handful of substances that expand on freezing. Its crystal structure is more
open than its liquid, so as the alloy solidifies the antimony pushes outward and offsets the
shrinkage of the lead around it. The casting stays dimensionally faithful, and the type comes out
of the mould with a crisp face. Antimony also forms hard crystals through the soft lead matrix,
which is what lets the face survive the press, and tin improves how cleanly the melt flows into the
mould before it sets.

<Forward year={1450}>
This alloy has an unusually long working life. Very nearly the same lead-tin-antimony recipe is
still being cast in Linotype machines in the 1970s, five hundred years after Mainz, which makes it
one of the longest-serving engineered materials on this road. It also sets a pattern the road keeps
repeating: the visible invention is a machine, and the thing that actually had to be solved is a
material property nobody outside the trade ever hears about. The next entry where an alloy decides
whether a machine is possible at all is the cylinder of the first steam engine <Modern>(<Ref

</Forward>

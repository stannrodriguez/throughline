<Before>
Guiding light along a transparent rod by total internal reflection is a nineteenth-century parlour
demonstration, and by the 1950s fibre bundles are being used for medical endoscopes over distances
of a metre or two. For communication the idea is hopeless. Ordinary optical glass loses about a
thousand decibels per kilometre, which means the signal is down by a factor of ten every three
metres. Telecommunications engineers looking for more capacity than copper can carry treat glass as
a non-starter and pursue hollow metal waveguides instead.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
In 1966 Charles Kao and George Hockham at Standard Telecommunication Laboratories publish an
analysis arguing that the loss is not a property of glass at all but of the iron, copper and other
transition metals dissolved in it, and that a sufficiently pure glass would lose under twenty
decibels a kilometre — which would make it usable. Most of the field disagrees. In 1970 Robert
Maurer, Donald Keck and Peter Schultz at Corning, working with fused silica made by vapour
deposition rather than melted from sand, reach seventeen decibels a kilometre. Within a decade the
figure is below one, and today's fibre loses less than 0.2.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="17 dB/km in 1970 · under 0.2 today · 1.55 µm window">
A fibre is a core of glass surrounded by cladding of slightly lower refractive index. Light meeting
that boundary at a shallow angle is totally internally reflected, so it stays inside the core and
follows the fibre around bends. That much was never in doubt.

What Kao identified is that attenuation has separable causes. Absorption by metal-ion impurities can
in principle be removed entirely, and vapour-phase manufacture does remove it, because the process
starts from a gas that can be purified rather than from a mineral that cannot. What remains is
Rayleigh scattering off the random density fluctuations frozen into the glass as it solidified, and
that falls steeply with wavelength — as the fourth power of it — which is why long-haul systems
settled on infrared around 1.55 micrometres, where scattering is low and the residual absorption
happens to be lowest too.

The capacity that results is difficult to overstate. A single fibre carries many wavelengths at
once, each an independent channel, and a pair of hair-thin strands moves more traffic than every
copper telephone cable ever laid.

<Forward year={1970}>
Fibre is what makes distance financially irrelevant for data, finishing the job the telegraph
started in 1844. The cost of moving a bit between continents falls until it stops appearing in
anyone's calculations, which is the precondition for everything built on the assumption that a
server's location does not matter. It is also the third entry in a row on this road where the
achievement is subtraction: not a new substance but the removal of what was in the old one

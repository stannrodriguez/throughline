<Before>
Every cutting tool since the Iron Age has been made of a harder version of the thing it cuts. The
best available in 1900 is high-speed steel, an alloy of tungsten and chromium developed by Taylor
and White, which holds its edge even when the tip glows red — a real advance, and still a steel.
That imposes a ceiling. Cutting generates heat at the tool tip, heat softens steel, and a softened
tool wears away. Machining speed is therefore limited not by the machine's power or rigidity but by
how hot the cutting edge is allowed to get, and a shop making engine blocks spends much of its time
regrinding tools.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Tungsten carbide, the compound of tungsten and carbon, has been known since the 1890s and is
extraordinarily hard and completely unworkable — it cannot be melted and cast at any practical
temperature, and on its own it shatters. The answer, patented by Karl Schröter in 1923 and brought
to market by Krupp in 1927 under the name Widia, from <em>wie Diamant</em>, is to give up on
casting: grind the carbide to a powder, mix it with powdered cobalt, press it to shape and heat
until the cobalt melts and flows around the grains without melting them. What comes out is a
composite — hard ceramic particles held in a tough metal binder.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="~1,800 HV against 700 · 3–4× cutting speed · Widia, 1927">
The two components fail in opposite ways and each covers for the other. Tungsten carbide is around
1,800 on the Vickers scale against roughly 700 for hardened steel, and it keeps that hardness at
temperatures where steel has gone soft, because the hardness comes from the covalent carbide
structure rather than from a heat treatment that can be undone. On its own it is brittle. Cobalt is
comparatively soft and very tough, and as a thin film between the grains it absorbs the shock and
arrests the cracks that would otherwise split the ceramic. Varying the cobalt fraction trades
hardness against toughness, which is how grades are specified.

The manufacturing method is as significant as the material. Sintering — forming a shape from powder
and consolidating it below the melting point — makes it possible to work with substances that
cannot be melted at all, and it becomes the standard route to technical ceramics, magnets and
refractory metals.

<Forward year={1927}>
Cutting speeds rise three- to fourfold, and machining ceases to set the pace of mass production
just as production volumes make that decisive. The wider point is the one the materials road makes
repeatedly from here on: the best material is often not a substance but an arrangement of two,
each supplying what the other lacks. Reinforced concrete does it with steel in compression-strong
stone; cemented carbide does it with ceramic in a metal binder; and the composites the aerospace
industry runs on do it with fibre in resin <Modern>(<Ref to="nylon-and-polymers">nylon and the
polymers</Ref>, P.X)</Modern>.
</Forward>

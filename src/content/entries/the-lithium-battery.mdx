<Before>
Portable electronics in the early 1980s are limited by what they are carrying. Lead-acid holds
about 35 watt-hours per kilogram, which is why a car battery weighs what it does. Nickel-cadmium
manages perhaps 50, at 1.2 volts a cell, so several are needed in series to run anything, and the
chemistry suffers from a memory effect that punishes partial recharging. Anything genuinely
portable therefore runs on disposable cells or on a cord. Attempts to build a rechargeable cell
around lithium metal itself — which is the lightest metal and the most electropositive, and would
be ideal — keep failing in the same way: on recharging, lithium plates back onto the electrode as
needles that grow across the cell, short it out and set it on fire.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
The answer is not to plate metallic lithium anywhere. John Goodenough's group showed in 1980 that
lithium cobalt oxide will give up lithium ions reversibly and serve as a cathode. In 1985 Akira
Yoshino at Asahi Kasei pairs it with an anode of carbonaceous material that will take those ions in
between its layers, so lithium is never present as a metal at either end — only as ions moving
between two hosts. Sony commercialises the design in 1991, and the cell arrives just as devices are
being designed that assume it.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="~150 Wh/kg against 35 · 3.6 V a cell · prototype 1985">
Two properties of lithium do the work. It is the lightest metal, so a given number of charge
carriers weighs very little; and it sits at the far end of the electrochemical series, so the
voltage available from a lithium reaction is high. A cell gives about 3.6 volts against 1.2 for
nickel-cadmium, and energy is voltage times charge, so the gain compounds. Practical cells reach
150 watt-hours per kilogram and better, four or more times lead-acid.

The engineering cost is that the chemistry is energetic in both directions. A cell that stores that
much in that little volume also releases it quickly if the separator fails, and the electrolyte is
flammable, so every pack carries protection circuitry that limits charge voltage, current and
temperature. The failures that make the news are almost always mechanical or manufacturing faults
that let the two electrodes meet.

<Forward year={1985}>
The cell is what makes a device untethered, and the list of things that follow from that is longer
than it looks: the laptop, the mobile phone, the cordless tool, the hearing aid, the implanted
defibrillator, the drone, and eventually the car. Its second act is larger still. Three decades of
manufacturing for consumer electronics drove the price down by roughly ninety percent, which is
what made it plausible to build the same chemistry at the scale of a power station <Modern>(<Ref
to="grid-scale-storage">grid-scale storage</Ref>, P.XII)</Modern>.
</Forward>

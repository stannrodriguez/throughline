<Before>
A blast furnace consumes charcoal, and charcoal is wood. A single furnace in continuous operation
eats the annual growth of several thousand hectares of coppice, so an ironworks is really a
forestry operation with a furnace attached, and its output is capped by how fast trees grow.
Britain by 1700 has been short of timber for a century, competing with shipbuilding and domestic
fuel, and iron production has effectively stalled — much of the country's iron is imported from
Sweden and Russia, where the forests are.

Britain is also sitting on a great deal of coal, and coal cannot be used. Its sulphur dissolves
into the iron and makes the metal crack when worked hot, a fault the trade calls red-shortness.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Abraham Darby, working at Coalbrookdale, succeeds in smelting iron with coke in 1709. Coke is coal
that has been baked in a heap or an oven without enough air to burn, driving off the volatile
compounds and most of the sulphur and leaving a strong porous carbon. The process is not adopted
quickly, because early coke iron is only reliable for castings and not for the forge, and charcoal
iron stays the premium product. It takes his son and grandson, better coking practice and the
selection of low-sulphur coals to make coke iron general, which it becomes through the 1750s and
1760s.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="Coalbrookdale 1709 · general by the 1760s · sulphur driven off">
Coking is the same operation as charcoal-burning, applied to a different feedstock. Heat coal
without air and the tars, gases and much of the sulphur come off as vapour, leaving a carbon
skeleton. What survives is stronger than charcoal, and that mechanical difference matters more than
the chemistry: a blast furnace is a column, and the fuel at the bottom carries the weight of
everything above it. Charcoal crushes under its own charge, which caps how tall a charcoal furnace
can be. Coke does not, so coke furnaces are built taller, run larger charges, and produce more iron
per day.

The remaining sulphur is dealt with by charging more limestone, which takes it into the slag. The
practical test is entirely empirical for a century: coals from some seams make good iron and coals
from others do not, and nobody can say why until analytical chemistry catches up.

<Forward year={1763}>
Two constraints come off at once. Iron output is no longer bounded by forest growth, so it can rise
with demand rather than with woodland; and the furnace no longer has to stand in a forest, so it
moves to the coalfields — Shropshire, South Wales, the Black Country, later the Ruhr — where the
coal, the iron ore and the limestone often lie within a few miles of each other. The industrial
geography of the next two centuries is drawn by that coincidence. The Iron Bridge at Coalbrookdale,
cast in 1779 from this furnace's metal, is the first structure to make the point in public.
</Forward>

<Before>
Every method of laying down a thin film sprays or grows material continuously, and its thickness is
therefore a function of time, rate and geometry. Evaporation and sputtering deposit along a line of
sight, so a surface facing away from the source receives less; chemical vapour deposition is better
but still depends on how much gas reaches each point. At a hundred nanometres these variations are
tolerable. Below about five nanometres they are not, because a film three atoms thick in one place
and eight in another is not a film with a thickness at all.

The problem becomes urgent in semiconductor manufacture around 2000. The insulating layer under a
transistor's gate has been shrinking with everything else, and silicon dioxide reaches about 1.2
nanometres — five atoms — at which point electrons tunnel straight through it and the transistor
leaks current whether it is switched on or not.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Atomic-layer deposition replaces the continuous process with a sequence of self-terminating ones.
Pulse in a precursor gas that reacts with the surface and not with itself: it covers the surface,
finds nothing left to react with, and stops. Purge the chamber. Pulse in a second precursor that
reacts with the first: it converts the layer and stops. Purge again. One cycle, one atomic layer,
roughly a tenth of a nanometre, wherever the gas can reach. Tuomo Suntola developed the method in
Finland in the 1970s for electroluminescent displays; it becomes central to chip manufacture from
the late 1990s, and Intel's 45-nanometre process of 2007 uses it to lay down hafnium oxide gate
insulators.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="~0.1 nm per cycle · self-limiting · high-k gates from 2007">
The self-limiting reaction is the entire idea, and it changes what determines the result. In every
other deposition method thickness depends on how long the process ran and how much material
arrived; here it depends on how many cycles were performed. Running a pulse twice as long deposits
nothing extra, because the surface is already saturated. Thickness becomes a count rather than a
measurement, and a count is exact.

The second consequence is conformality. Because the reaction proceeds wherever the gas reaches and
stops when the surface is covered, the film is the same thickness at the bottom of a hole a hundred
times deeper than it is wide as it is on the flat. That is what makes the three-dimensional
structures in modern chips — deep trenches, fins, stacked gates — manufacturable at all.

Hafnium oxide matters because it has a higher dielectric constant than silicon dioxide, so a
physically thicker layer produces the same electrical effect. The tunnelling that killed the thin
oxide depends on physical thickness, and the gate's function depends on the electrical equivalent,
so a high-k material buys back both.

<Forward year={1995}>
This entry closes the argument the materials road has been making since Period VII. Wilkinson bored
a cylinder true to a millimetre and a half in 1774 and that was the finest tolerance in the world;
this places matter to a tenth of a nanometre, about seven million times finer, and does it over a
whole wafer at once. The road's three units in order are temperature, then purity, then placement —
how hot, then what is in it, then exactly where each layer sits — and each replaced the one before
it when it stopped separating one period from the next.
</Forward>

<Before>
Latitude is easy: measure the sun's height at noon and read it off a table. Longitude at sea is
unsolvable, and the cost is ships. In 1707 four Royal Navy vessels ground onto the Scilly rocks in
fog and roughly fourteen hundred sailors drown, because the fleet's best navigators had misjudged
their position by leagues. Parliament responds by posting the Longitude Prize: £20,000 — a fortune
— for a method good to half a degree.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Longitude is just time. The Earth turns fifteen degrees an hour, so a clock carrying home-port time
lets a navigator compare it against local noon and read longitude directly off the difference. The
problem is that no clock survives a ship: pendulums are useless on a rolling deck, and metals
expand and contract with every change of climate. John Harrison, a self-taught joiner, spends four
decades on the problem. His fourth attempt, H4, looks like an oversized pocket watch and loses five
seconds in eighty-one days on the passage to Jamaica.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="15° / hr · 5 Hz · 5 s in 81 days">
A clock is an oscillator plus an energy feed, and every error is something perturbing the
oscillator's period. Harrison's solutions attack each perturbation physically rather than
correcting for it afterward. A bimetallic strip bends with temperature to shorten the balance
spring by exactly as much as heat lengthens it. A high-frequency balance beating five times a
second makes the ship's motion slow compared to the oscillation, so the roll of the deck averages
out instead of accumulating. A remontoire keeps the watch running at constant force even while it
is being wound.

The arithmetic is what makes the achievement legible. Each second of clock error is a quarter
nautical mile of position error at the equator. Five seconds in eighty-one days means H4 crossed
the Atlantic and knew where it was to within about two kilometers — an order of magnitude inside
the prize's half-degree, and a standard no ship's navigator had ever been able to assume.

<Forward year={1761}>
A watch that knows the hour in Greenwich while it sits in the Caribbean is a curiosity to everyone
except the men who have to find harbours in it. It will not stay a curiosity: the Admiralty will
buy them by the hundred, and within a lifetime no ship of consequence will sail without one. What
follows is not faster shipping but *certain* shipping — the same hulls, the same sails, on routes
that can be surveyed once and trusted afterward <Modern>(the charts and trade networks of <Ref
to="coke-smelted-iron">the next period</Ref>)</Modern>. And the instrument-making trade that
learned to compensate a spring for heat will shortly be asked to bore a cylinder true
<Modern>(<Ref to="wilkinsons-boring-machine">Wilkinson's boring machine</Ref>, P.VII)</Modern>: the
same hands, a different constraint.
</Forward>

<Before>
Steel is iron with a small and specific amount of carbon in it — enough to harden on quenching, not
so much that it becomes brittle cast iron. Getting there in 1740 means the cementation process:
pack wrought iron bars with charcoal in a sealed chest and hold them at red heat for a week, so
carbon diffuses in from the surface. The result is blister steel, named for the blisters that form
on it, and it is carburised unevenly by definition — carbon-rich at the skin and nearly pure iron
at the core. Smiths correct for this by folding and forge-welding the bars repeatedly, which
averages the composition out and takes a great deal of skill and time. Every batch is slightly
different, which is tolerable for a knife and disqualifying for a clock spring.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Benjamin Huntsman, a clockmaker in Doncaster dissatisfied with the steel available for springs and
pendulums, works out how to melt it. Around 1740 in Handsworth he builds coke-fired furnaces that
hold small clay crucibles above 1,600°, hot enough to liquefy steel, with the pot lidded so the
metal is never exposed to the furnace gases. Molten steel mixes, so whatever carbon is in the charge
distributes itself evenly, and the slag and inclusions float clear. The Sheffield trade at first
refuses the product as too hard to work, and the process becomes general there through the 1760s
and 1770s.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="1,600° · sealed crucible · uniform carbon">
The entry turns on one property of the liquid state: a melt is homogeneous, and a solid worked at
the forge is only as homogeneous as the smith's folding made it. Once the steel has been fully
molten, composition is something the maker sets by weighing the charge instead of something the
metal inherits from how deeply carbon happened to diffuse.

The temperature is the hard part. Steel melts near 1,500°, and holding a crucible above that in a
coke fire needs a refractory clay that survives the heat without slumping or contaminating the
charge — Huntsman's clay recipe was the part he kept secret. The sealed lid matters just as much,
because an open melt would pick up carbon from the fuel and lose it to the air unpredictably, which
is exactly the variability the process exists to remove.

The output is small. A crucible holds perhaps twenty kilograms, and a furnace runs a few crucibles
at a time, so cast steel stays expensive and reserved for things where consistency justifies the
price: springs, cutting tools, files, razors, and later the dies and taps that make other machines.

<Forward year={1770}>
Uniform steel is what makes precision machinery possible, because a tool that cuts metal has to be
harder and more predictable than the metal it cuts. Sheffield becomes a world centre for cutting
tools on this process and stays one for a century and a half. What crucible steel never becomes is
cheap: twenty kilograms a pot will not build a railway, a bridge or a ship, so structural work
carries on in wrought and cast iron until somebody finds a way to make steel in tonne quantities
<Modern>(<Ref to="bessemer-steel">Bessemer steel</Ref>, P.VIII)</Modern>.
</Forward>

<Before>
A mechanical clock before 1656 runs on a verge escapement with a foliot — a weighted bar that
rocks back and forth, its rate set by where the weights are slid along it. Nothing about the
arrangement fixes the period: the rate depends on how much force the driving weight happens to be
applying, which changes as the clock unwinds, and on friction, which changes with dirt and
temperature. A good tower clock of the period gains or loses about fifteen minutes a day. Clocks
are built with one hand because a minute hand would be dishonest.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Galileo noticed around 1602 that a swinging lamp appears to take the same time per swing whether
the swing is wide or narrow, and late in life sketched a clock using the effect; nothing working
came of it. In 1656 Christiaan Huygens builds one: a pendulum, driven and counted by an escapement,
with the weight-driven train supplying only enough energy to replace what friction removes. He
patents it in 1657 and publishes the full theory in <em>Horologium Oscillatorium</em> in 1673.
Error drops to roughly fifteen seconds a day at once, and to about a second a day within eighty
years, once George Graham's deadbeat escapement and temperature-compensated pendulums arrive.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="T = 2π√(L/g) · 0.994 m for a second · 15 min → 15 s">
The period of a small swing is 2π times the square root of the length over gravity, and neither the
weight of the bob nor the width of the swing appears in it. That independence is what makes a
pendulum a standard rather than merely a regulator: the driving force can vary, the amplitude can
decay, and the rate stays put. A pendulum 0.994 metres long ticks once a second, which is why
longcase clocks are the height they are.

The independence is only approximate. A pendulum swinging in a circular arc takes very slightly
longer at wider amplitudes, and Huygens showed that a bob constrained to a cycloidal path would be
exactly isochronous, fitting curved cheeks at the suspension to bend the arc. In practice the fix
that matters is keeping the amplitude small and constant, which is what a good escapement does.

The remaining errors are all physical inputs to the same formula. Heat lengthens the rod, so the
clock runs slow in summer, which gridiron and mercury pendulums correct by combining metals that
expand differently. And gravity itself varies with latitude, a fact discovered when a pendulum
clock taken to Cayenne in 1672 lost time — the first measurement of the Earth's shape made with a
clock.

<Forward year={1656}>
Fifteen seconds a day makes the minute hand worth fitting and the second hand worth inventing, and
it turns timing into an experimental instrument: rates of fall, the speed of sound, the transits of
stars. It also creates the standard that the rest of this period fails to meet at sea. A pendulum
depends on gravity pulling steadily in one direction, and a deck that rolls, pitches and heaves
supplies exactly the opposite, which is why every serious person believes a seagoing clock is
impossible right up until one crosses the Atlantic <Modern>(<Ref to="marine-chronometer">the marine
chronometer</Ref>, P.VI)</Modern>.
</Forward>

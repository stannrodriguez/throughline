<Before>
The telegraph works and it is not a conversation. A message is written out, handed to a clerk,
encoded into pulses, tapped along the wire, decoded by another clerk, written out again and
delivered by a boy. Each step costs time and money and each is a place where the message can be
garbled or read by someone else. Traffic is therefore terse and expensive, priced by the word, and
the network is a business tool rather than something in a house. The obvious improvement people are
chasing in the 1870s is not speech at all — it is the harmonic telegraph, a way of sending several
Morse messages down one wire at different pitches.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Alexander Graham Bell, working on exactly that multiplexing problem with an understanding of
acoustics from teaching deaf students, files a patent on 14 February 1876 covering the transmission
of vocal sounds by undulating current. Elisha Gray files a caveat describing a similar liquid
transmitter the same day, hours later, and the priority is litigated for years. On 10 March Bell's
apparatus carries a sentence to Thomas Watson in the next room. Early instruments are barely
audible; what makes the telephone commercially practical is the carbon microphone developed by
David Hughes and Thomas Edison, in which sound pressure varies the resistance of carbon granules
and modulates a battery current far stronger than a diaphragm could generate on its own.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="14 February 1876 · analogue current · no code to learn">
The difference from the telegraph is the difference between discrete and continuous. A telegraph
key is a switch: the wire is either carrying current or not, and meaning lives in the pattern of
those states. A telephone transmitter makes the current an analogue of the sound pressure at the
diaphragm — rising and falling in step with it, at the same frequencies — and the receiver turns
that varying current back into motion of another diaphragm. Nothing is encoded, so nothing has to
be decoded, and the information carried is whatever the ear can hear rather than whatever the code
can express.

The cost is that the signal is fragile in a way a coded one is not. A telegraph pulse only has to
be distinguishable from no pulse, so it can be regenerated cleanly at every relay. An analogue
waveform degrades continuously and every amplifier adds noise, which is why long-distance telephony
has to wait for the vacuum tube in the 1910s and why the transatlantic telephone cable comes ninety
years after the transatlantic telegraph cable.

<Forward year={1876}>
The telephone is the first communication technology that ordinary people operate themselves,
which is why it ends up in houses when the telegraph never did. It also creates the switching
problem: a network where any subscriber may want to reach any other cannot be wired point to point,
so it needs exchanges, and exchanges need either operators or automation. That problem — routing a
connection through a shared network on demand — is the one the next century keeps solving over
again, in switchboards, then in electromechanical exchanges, and finally by giving up on
connections entirely and sending addressed fragments <Modern>(<Ref to="the-internet">the
internet</Ref>, P.XI)</Modern>.
</Forward>

<Before>
Two hundred kilometres a day, set by the Persian relay, has been the rate for twenty-three
centuries. Chappe's semaphore beats it and works only in daylight and clear air, which in northern
Europe rules out a large part of the year. Everything else that has happened to information —
paper, the alphabet, printing — has changed how many copies exist or how cheaply they are made.
None of it has changed the speed, because every message is still an object that has to be carried
from one place to another by something that moves.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Several people build working electric telegraphs in the late 1830s: Cooke and Wheatstone's
five-needle instrument goes into service on the Great Western Railway in 1839. The version that
spreads is Samuel Morse's, with Alfred Vail, because it needs only a single wire and one moving
part at each end, and because its code is efficient — the commonest letters get the shortest
symbols. On 24 May 1844 the Washington–Baltimore line is opened with a message from the Capitol.
Submarine cables follow: the Channel in 1851, and after one failure in 1858, a permanent Atlantic
cable in 1866.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="24 May 1844 · ~30 words a minute · 1866 transatlantic">
Nothing physical makes the journey. A current in a wire propagates near the speed of light, so the
transit time over any terrestrial distance is negligible and the message's delay is entirely the
operators at the two ends: a skilled hand sends around thirty words a minute.

Two engineering problems shape the system. The first is attenuation over long lines, solved by
relays — an incoming signal too weak to work a sounder is used instead to close a local circuit
with its own battery, regenerating the signal at full strength. That is repeatable indefinitely,
which is why distance becomes a question of how many repeaters rather than how strong a signal. The
second is that a submarine cable's capacitance smears each pulse into the next, which is what
destroyed the 1858 Atlantic cable when its operators applied high voltage to force signals through.
The 1866 cable succeeds by sending gently and detecting sensitively, with Thomson's mirror
galvanometer reading currents a fraction of what the earlier equipment needed.

<Forward year={1844}>
The consequences arrive faster than anyone plans for. Railways can run trains safely at intervals
because a station can tell the next station what has left. Prices in distant markets converge
within days rather than months, and commodity exchanges reorganise around the fact. Newspapers
begin printing news that happened yesterday somewhere else, and the wire services are built to
supply it. Empires are administered on a timescale of hours. It also creates the first
international standard-setting bodies, because a message crossing borders needs agreed codes and
tariffs, and the organisation founded for that in 1865 still exists. What the telegraph does not
carry is a voice, and reproducing one takes a different kind of signal <Modern>(<Ref
to="telephone">the telephone</Ref>, same era)</Modern>.
</Forward>

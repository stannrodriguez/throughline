<Before>
A Watt engine is superbly economical and enormous. It works at two to five pounds per square inch
above atmosphere, which means a large cylinder is needed to develop useful force, which means a
heavy beam, a heavy frame and a masonry house to stand in. The condenser and its cold water add
more. None of this matters for a mine or a mill, where the engine is built once and never moves.
It rules out the application everyone can see coming — an engine that carries itself and a load
along a road or a rail — because the engine weighs more than anything it could pull.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Richard Trevithick, a Cornish mine engineer, patents a high-pressure engine in 1802 and builds
them at around fifty pounds per square inch, ten times Watt's working pressure. At that pressure
the steam does its work by expansion and can then be exhausted straight up the chimney, so the
condenser, the cold-water supply and the air pump all disappear. In February 1804 one of his
engines on the Penydarren tramroad in South Wales hauls ten tons of iron and seventy men about
fifteen kilometres, which is the first steam locomotive to do real work on rails.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="~50 psi against 5 · 10 tons hauled 15 km · 1804">
Force on a piston is pressure times area, so raising the pressure tenfold lets the area fall
tenfold for the same push. Everything scales down with it: cylinder, frame, foundation. Discarding
the condenser removes the heaviest remaining component and the water it needs, at a cost in
efficiency — exhausting to atmosphere throws away steam that a condenser would have recovered.
The engine gets worse per pound of coal and far better per pound of engine, and for anything that
has to move itself the second ratio is the only one that matters.

Exhausting up the chimney turns out to have a second effect nobody designed. The jet of spent
steam draws air through the firebox, so the fire burns harder the harder the engine works. That
blast pipe is what makes a locomotive boiler produce steam fast enough to be useful.

The danger Watt warned about is real. Boilers of the period are riveted wrought iron of uncertain
quality with no reliable gauge, and Trevithick's Greenwich engine explodes in 1803 and kills four
men. The technology's history for the next half century is a running argument between pressure and
metallurgy.

<Forward year={1802}>
Trevithick dies poor and the locomotive belongs to other people, but the ratio he establishes —
power against weight — is the one the whole energy road optimises from here. It is what puts an
engine on a rail in the 1820s, on a ship without a paddle in the 1840s, and eventually in the air.
Each step is the same move repeated: get the working pressure up and the dead weight out. The
limit of doing that with an external fire and a boiler is reached when somebody moves the
combustion inside the cylinder <Modern>(<Ref to="internal-combustion">internal combustion</Ref>,
P.IX)</Modern>.
</Forward>

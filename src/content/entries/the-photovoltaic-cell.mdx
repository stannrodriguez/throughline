<Before>
Every energy source on this road works by heating something. Fire, coal, oil, fission — all of them
raise a temperature, boil a fluid and push a piston or a blade, and all of them are bound by the
thermodynamic ceiling that a heat engine's efficiency depends on the temperature difference it
works across. Water and wind avoid the fire and still work through a moving machine that wears out.
The photoelectric effect has been known since Hertz noticed it in 1887 and explained by Einstein in
1905, but selenium cells made on that principle convert about one percent of the light hitting
them, which is a laboratory curiosity rather than a power source.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller and Gerald Pearson at Bell Labs, working with the silicon and the
doping techniques the transistor programme had developed, demonstrate a cell in April 1954 that
converts about six percent of incident sunlight to electricity. It is announced as a device that
could power rural telephone equipment. The economics are hopeless — around $286 a watt in 1956,
against a few cents for grid power — and the first serious application is the one where no cable can
reach and cost is not the point: the satellite Vanguard 1, launched in 1958, carries six cells and
transmits for six years after its chemical battery dies.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="6% in 1954 · $286 a watt in 1956 · no moving part">
A photon with more energy than the semiconductor's band gap can knock an electron loose, leaving a
mobile electron and a mobile vacancy. In an undifferentiated crystal they would simply recombine.
The junction between p-type and n-type silicon carries a built-in electric field that sweeps the
two in opposite directions before they can, so a current flows out of the cell whenever light falls
on it. There is no heat cycle, no working fluid and nothing that moves, which is why a panel with
no maintenance runs for decades.

The efficiency limit is set by the band gap being a single number. Photons with less energy than
the gap pass through and do nothing; photons with much more lose the excess as heat within
picoseconds. That trade-off puts the theoretical ceiling for a single-junction silicon cell around
33 percent, and commercial modules now reach the mid-twenties.

What has actually changed since 1954 is not the physics but the price, which has fallen by roughly
three orders of magnitude, largely through the same manufacturing learning curve that drove down
the cost of chips.

<Forward year={1954}>
A photovoltaic panel is the first entry on this road that produces power without heat, without
motion and without fuel, and its economics are the opposite of everything before it: almost all the
cost is paid at installation and the fuel is free forever after. That inverts how generation is
planned, and it introduces the constraint the road ends on. Sunlight arrives when it arrives, in
quantities that do not follow demand, and a grid that has matched supply to demand instant by
instant since 1882 has to acquire somewhere to put the surplus <Modern>(<Ref
to="grid-scale-storage">grid-scale storage</Ref>, P.XII)</Modern>.
</Forward>

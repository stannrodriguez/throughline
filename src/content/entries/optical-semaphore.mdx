<Before>
Two hundred kilometres a day, by relayed horse, is where the number has stood since the Persian
royal road. Everything since has changed how many copies of a message exist and nothing has changed
how fast one travels. For a republic at war on several frontiers in 1793 this is an acute
operational problem: an order from Paris to the northern army takes the best part of two days, and
the reply takes as long again, so the government is always acting on a picture of the front that
is four days old.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
Claude Chappe builds a chain of towers within sight of each other, each carrying a pivoted
crossbeam with a hinged arm at either end. The positions of the three members encode a symbol, and
an operator with a telescope reads the next tower along and repeats what it shows. The Paris–Lille
line opens in 1794: 230 kilometres, twenty-two stations, and a message that arrives in minutes
rather than days. The first dispatch carried announces the recapture of Condé-sur-l'Escaut, in
Paris within an hour of the event. The network grows to more than five hundred stations and some
five thousand kilometres by the 1840s.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="230 km · 22 stations · 2–3 symbols a minute">
The design problem is not distance but error. Each station copies what it sees and passes it on, so
a misread is propagated faithfully down the whole line. Chappe's answer is to make the symbol set
small and visually distinct — a few dozen positions of the arms, each unmistakable at fifteen
kilometres through a telescope — and then to buy back the expressive power with a codebook, where
pairs of symbols index a page and a line in a dictionary of phrases. That keeps transmissions short,
which limits both the time on the line and the chances of error, and it encrypts the traffic as a
side effect: an observer who can see the arms perfectly still needs the book.

Throughput is low by any later standard, two or three symbols a minute, and the system's real
weakness is meteorological. It needs daylight and clear air, so it does not work at night, in fog,
in rain or in snow, which in northern France is a substantial fraction of the year. The towers also
cost a great deal to build and require an operator awake in every one of them, which is why the
network carries government traffic almost exclusively.

<Forward year={1793}>
For fifty years this is the fastest information moves anywhere, and it establishes something more
durable than the towers: that a message can be separated from any object carrying it, and passed
along as a pattern that gets copied at each step. Every telecommunication system afterwards is a
version of that idea with a better medium. The weather is what kills it. A signal that travels
along a wire does not care whether it is dark or foggy, and within twenty years of its arrival
France abandons the semaphore network entirely <Modern>(<Ref to="electric-telegraph">the electric
telegraph</Ref>, P.VIII)</Modern>.
</Forward>

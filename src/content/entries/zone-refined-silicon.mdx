<Before>
Bardeen and Brattain's transistor works and cannot be manufactured reliably. Devices made from
adjacent pieces of the same germanium behave differently, some barely work, and yields are dismal.
The reason is that a semiconductor's electrical behaviour is set by impurities at concentrations
far below anything conventional metallurgy bothers to measure. Chemically pure material — 99.99
percent, which is excellent by any 1950 standard — still contains one foreign atom in ten thousand,
and that is around a million times more contamination than the intended doping. Every metallurgical
technique available is designed to remove percent-level impurities, and none of them can go
further.
</Before>

<Breakthrough>
William Pfann at Bell Labs publishes zone refining in 1952. A narrow band of the ingot is melted by
a heater ring and the ring is moved slowly from one end to the other, so the molten zone travels
the length of the bar. Most impurities are more soluble in the liquid than in the solid, so as the
zone advances they stay dissolved in it and are dragged along, concentrating at the far end. Run the
pass repeatedly — the apparatus can do several zones at once — and the impurities pile up at one
end, which is cut off and discarded. The technique reaches parts per billion, and combined with
Czochralski crystal pulling gives a single crystal of that purity with no grain boundaries in it at
all.
</Breakthrough>

<Science numbers="<1 part per billion · segregation coefficient · 1952">
The whole method rests on one number per impurity: the segregation coefficient, the ratio of how
much of that element sits in the freezing solid against how much stays in the liquid it freezes
from. For most contaminants in silicon that ratio is well below one, meaning the solidifying crystal
rejects them. Each pass of the molten zone therefore leaves the bar behind it slightly cleaner than
it was, and the effect compounds with every pass. It is a distillation carried out inside a solid,
and it is the same physics as ice freezing fresh out of salt water.

Elements whose coefficient is near one — boron in silicon is the awkward case — are barely removed
at all, and must be dealt with chemically before the ingot is grown.

The scale is difficult to state usefully. One part per billion is one second in thirty-two years,
or one grain of sand in a cubic metre of them. It is a purity no material on this road before now
had any reason to reach, and it is not an incremental improvement on anything: it is four or five
orders of magnitude past where metallurgy had been.

<Forward year={1952}>
This is where the materials road's unit changes for good. Temperature ruled from Period I to Period
IX, and past 3,500° there was nothing left worth melting; what replaces it is purity, and this
entry is the first one measured in nines. Everything the information road does afterwards sits on
top of it, because an integrated circuit is a pattern of intended impurities in a crystal and every
unintended one is a defect. The doubling that carries computing for fifty years is only possible
because the substrate is clean enough that the same pattern can be printed a billion times and work
each time.
</Forward>
